This is the second half of the beginner-API pair. [Tutorial 3](tutorial3-hst009.md)
nested three spheres in water. Here we nest **five**, put them in a **cube of
graphite**, and put a **mirror** on the cube.

## The question

**What is the multiplication factor of a lattice of fuel particles, and how
do I describe one particle and let the computer repeat it?**

A TRISO particle is a fuel kernel with four coating layers. The one in the
HTR-10 pebble bed
([Rung 5](triso.md) tells the reactor's story) has, from the centre
outwards:

| layer | outer radius [cm] | material | density [g/cm³] |
|---|---|---|---|
| kernel | 0.0250 | UO2, 17 wt % U-235, + 4 ppm natural B per U atom | 10.4 |
| buffer | 0.0340 | porous carbon, + 1.3 ppm B per C atom | 1.1 |
| IPyC (inner pyrolytic carbon) | 0.0380 | pyrolytic carbon, + 1.3 ppm B per C atom | 1.9 |
| SiC | 0.0415 | silicon carbide | 3.18 |
| OPyC (outer pyrolytic carbon) | 0.0455 | pyrolytic carbon, + 1.3 ppm B per C atom | 1.9 |
| matrix (the cube) | side 0.19876 | graphite, + 1.3 ppm B per C atom | 1.73 |

**Where each number comes from.** Radii: IAEA-TECDOC-1382 part 2, which states
the coating thicknesses twice in two units (0.09 / 0.04 / 0.035 / 0.04 mm),
and Li, Yu & Wei (2014) Table 2 agrees (the doc of
`TrisoRadii::HTR10`). Compositions: `outram_mc_libs::pebble_beds::htr10`
(TECDOC-1382 Table 4-2 and Li 2014 Table 2). Boron is natural boron by *atom*
ppm (gh:#424), on every graphite layer and not on SiC.

**The boron basis is the lesson inside the lesson.** "4 ppm boron" means
nothing until it says *per what*. In the kernel it is per uranium atom
(`PpmOf::HeavyMetal`); in graphite it is per carbon atom
(`PpmOf::PerHostAtom`, "atoms per carbon atom" said plainly). An unstated ppm
basis once cost this project a wrong reading; the API makes you say it.

## What you write

The particle, kernel outwards, **one `.containing` per statement**, exactly as
in tutorial 3:

```rust,ignore
let kernel = Sphere::radius(cm(r.kernel)).filled_with(uo2).in_component(kernel_c);
let buffer_ball = Sphere::radius(cm(r.buffer)).filled_with(buffer)
    .in_component(coatings_c).containing(kernel);
let ipyc = Sphere::radius(cm(r.ipyc)).filled_with(pyc.clone())
    .in_component(coatings_c).containing(buffer_ball);
let sic_ball = Sphere::radius(cm(r.sic)).filled_with(sic)
    .in_component(coatings_c).containing(ipyc);
let opyc = Sphere::radius(cm(r.opyc)).filled_with(pyc)
    .in_component(coatings_c).containing(sic_ball);
```

([`Shape::containing`](../../code-review/#crates/outram-mc-libs/src/model/shape.rs::Shape::containing).)
The materials are bound media: graphite layers carry
`.bound_in(Moderator::Graphite(GraphiteKind::ReactorPorosity30))`, SiC carries
`.bound_in(Moderator::SiliconCarbide)` (Si in SiC and C in SiC), and the kernel
is a free gas. All thermal laws are regenerated by LEAPR from the ENDF/B-VIII.0
decks. (The 30 % porosity graphite is the HTR-10 choice recorded on
`nee_soon`'s `GraphiteLaw`, made on density, not to match k.)

**A cube sized by a packing fraction.** HTR-10 has 8335 particles in a pebble's
2.5 cm fuel zone, a packing fraction $8335 (0.0455/2.5)^3 = 0.050248$ (5.0248 %).
Ask for a cube that gives one particle exactly that fraction:

```rust,ignore
let cube = Cube::with_packing_fraction(&opyc, Ratio::new::<ratio>(pf));
let unit_cell = cube.filled_with(matrix.clone()).in_component(matrix_c).containing(opyc);
model.add(unit_cell.clone().boundary(Boundary::Reflective));
```

The cube's side is $a = (V_\text{particle} / \text{pf})^{1/3} = 0.19876$ cm
([`Cube::with_packing_fraction`](../../code-review/#crates/outram-mc-libs/src/model/shape.rs::Cube::with_packing_fraction)).
`Cube::side(cm(a))` gives the same cube by its length. The mirror is set on
the **outermost shape** with `.boundary(Boundary::Reflective)`; vacuum stays the
automatic default.

**What a mirrored cube means.** A neutron that reaches a face comes back
as if it had entered the next cube. One particle in a mirrored cube *is* an
infinite simple-cubic array of identical particles. Nothing leaks, so the
result is a **k-infinity**, not a k-effective.

**Repeating the cell.** `Lattice::copies_of(unit_cell, [3, 3, 3])` stacks 27
copies, the repeated structure that tutorials 5 to 8 build on. The example
draws and counts the stack but does not run it
([`Lattice::copies_of`](../../code-review/#crates/outram-mc-libs/src/model/lattice.rs::Lattice::copies_of)).

**Run size.** The presets (from `src/model/run.rs`):

| preset | per cycle × active cycles | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `MCSettings::Smoke` | 1 000 × 50 | does it run at all |
| `MCSettings::Fast` | 5 000 × 120 | a smoke test; **this example's default** |
| `MCSettings::Auto` | 10 000 × 250 | a quotable run (`--auto`; hours here) |
| `MCSettings::High` | 50 000 × 1 500 | a tight σ |

Runs **print OpenMC-style progress by default**; `KeffSettings::silent()`
turns it off.

```text

cargo run --release -p outram-mc-libs --example tutorial_triso_particle            # Fast (smoke test)
```

## Counting from the geometry

`assembled.census()` counts **what the solver will see, by walking the
assembled geometry**, not what you typed
([`AssembledModel::census`](../../code-review/#crates/outram-mc-libs/src/model/census.rs::AssembledModel::census)).
Why it exists: in the HTR-10 history a lattice held 8240 particles while the
model reported 8340 (gh:#316, +353 pcm). Results (2026-10-18):

| check | result |
|---|---|
| shells | the five shells (surfaces, regions, fills) are identical to `build_triso_particle(TrisoRadii::HTR10)`, the hand-built CSG |
| particles | exactly 1, at packing fraction 5.024811 % as asked (to 1e-12); kernel 0.83350 % of the volume |
| 3 × 3 × 3 stack | 27 particles, same packing fraction |
| transport | `tests/model_repeated_structures.rs`: the single-thread power iteration on the hand-built geometry and the beginner run on the assembled model give **the same k in every generation, to the last bit** (on the embedded LOW-tier data, so it tests the geometry, not the data) |
| census vs stochastic volume | the kernel volume agrees with an independent stochastic estimate within a 4σ band; analytic packing fractions agree within 1e-15 relative |

The bit-for-bit result is the strongest one on the page. It says the beginner
API's lowering adds nothing and loses nothing relative to the hand-built TRISO
universe. It does **not** say the model is physically right.

## What you get

Seed 1, `MCSettings::Fast`, ENDF/B-VIII.0 from `reference-data/endf/`,
2026-10-18, on one Intel i9-13900K shared with other jobs (pinned to 4 cores),
CPU only, as recorded in the example's doc comment. Two runs gave identical
bits.

| quantity | value | status |
|---|---|---|
| **k-infinity** | 1.56384 ± 0.00169 (batch means 0.00137) | **smoke test, not to quote** |

> **Data vintage.** These results were run on the route-4 data from **before**
> gh:#931 (PURR 20 bins × 16 ladders × 2 000 samples, no HEATR), on a branch that
> had not yet merged #931 (checked in git, 2026-10-18). The beginner API now
> builds nuclides through `process` at its defaults (64 × 10 000, HEATR first),
> so a re-run would differ by the #931 shift, which was not resolved for the
> benchmarks (e.g. HST-009 −40 ± 21 pcm). Not re-run.
| η, f, p, ε | 2.0244, 0.9777, 0.6596, 1.1964 | from the same smoke run |
| P_FNL, P_TNL | 1.00000 ± 0.00000 | both exactly 1: nothing can leak |
| six-factor product − k | **−186 ± 193 pcm** (−0.96σ) | consistent with 0 |
| thermal flux (below 0.625 eV) | 18.5 % of the kernel's flux, 18.9 % of the matrix's | peaks per lethargy near 0.08 to 0.10 eV |

Read them in this order.

1. **No leakage.** $P_{FNL} = P_{TNL} = 1$ is a *check*, not a result: every
   face is a mirror, so if either were below 1 the model would be wrong.
2. **The six-factor product reproduces k.** Written once more,
   $k = \eta f p \varepsilon P_{FNL} P_{TNL}$; with the last two equal to 1 the
   four factors alone give $1.5620 \pm 0.0009$ against $k = 1.5638$. The −186 pcm is under one
   sigma of the combined (and conservatively uncorrelated) uncertainty.
3. **Most of the flux is not thermal**: about 18.5 % is below 0.625 eV in
   the kernel and 18.9 % in the matrix. The prediction "mostly thermal" would
   have been wrong for this cell. It takes about 115 collisions in carbon to
   thermalise a neutron ($\ln(2\times10^{6}/0.025)/\xi$ with $\xi \approx 0.158$ for
   carbon, an estimate), and this cell is 5 % particle by volume with strongly
   absorbing U-235 in it. The thermal peak sits near 0.08 to 0.10 eV, a
   Maxwellian hardened by absorption, not at 0.025 eV. This is a smoke run,
   so read the 18.5 % as "roughly a fifth", not as a value to two figures.
4. **k-infinity above 1** is not "the HTR-10 is supercritical". This cell has
   no fuel-free pebble shell, no dummy graphite balls, no reflector and no
   leakage. **No published HTR-10 number is this quantity.** The nearest
   record in the crate, `examples/htr10_fuel_zone_kinf.rs`, is a randomly
   packed fuel zone with smeared coatings and different densities, a
   different problem, and it was not compared.

### Why it is slow: about 5 ms per history

| item | measured |
|---|---|
| data processing | 64 to 69 s (13 nuclides, 3 LEAPR laws, in parallel) |
| transport with diagnostics, Fast, one core (before #955) | **3764 to 3884 s** |
| per generation | about 5.5 s at 1 000 neutrons, 27 s at 5 000 |
| per history | roughly 5 ms |
| Auto (`--auto`) | about 3.8 h at that rate; a first Auto run was stopped at 1 h 38 min and gave no number |

The reason is geometric. A neutron in graphite has a mean free path of a few
cm; the cube is 0.199 cm wide, with mirrors. In about 115 collisions to
thermalise in carbon, a history **crosses or reflects off the faces thousands of
times**. Each crossing is a surface event, and the cost is in the surfaces,
not in the physics. ~~(The beginner run is single-threaded, gh:#955.) The
cheaper route to more statistics is independent seeds on several cores, without
diagnostics: `--seeds-only N`. That was not run for this record.~~
**CORRECTED 2026-10-18 (gh:#955):** the beginner run is now multi-threaded by
default, and the same seed gives the same k on any number of threads, bit for
bit (the per-history random stream depends only on seed, generation and
history). Measured on this cell, Fast with diagnostics: 1390 s on 4 threads
(2.3×) and 961 s on 6 threads (3.4×), against about 3800 s on one; k_inf =
1.56433 ± 0.00139, 49 pcm (0.2σ) from the single-thread record, because the
random streams differ. `KeffSettings::single_thread()` keeps the old path.

## Every simplification

| simplification | kind | what it is worth |
|---|---|---|
| A regular simple-cubic array, where the HTR-10 is randomly packed | open | not measured here; random packing is tutorial 5 |
| No fuel-free pebble shell, dummy balls, reflector or leakage | bounding by design | the result is a k-infinity of the cell, not an HTR-10 number |
| 293.6 K everywhere (room temperature of tutorials 1 to 3, not an HTR-10 operating state) | open | not estimated |
| 30 % porosity graphite law | chosen on density, not on k | no ablation of it here |
| 120 active cycles of 5 000 | too expensive | the reason this k is a smoke test, not quotable |
| The packing fraction comes from 8335 particles in a 2.5 cm zone | no data | TECDOC-1382 and Li 2014; the pebble shell is outside the zone |

## What we assumed

- That a mirrored cube around one particle is an infinite regular array
  (true of a reflective cube; checked by the census and the lattice test).
- That the quoted radii, densities and boron contents are right.
- That the graphite law chosen on density is the right law for every graphite
  layer, the buffer (density 1.1) included.
- That 120 cycles is enough to see whether the model runs; it is not enough
  to say what k is.
- Nothing was tuned, and no input was changed after seeing k.

## Use, modify, create

**Use.** Run `--geometry-only` and open the zoom on each layer. **Modify.**
Change the packing fraction (`Cube::with_packing_fraction` takes a ratio) and
re-run `--geometry-only` to see the cube grow or shrink around the particle:
what does the census report? **Create.** Describe a different coated particle
(a different number of layers) and count it.

## Next question

One particle in a mirror is a *regular* array. The pebble in the real reactor
has **thousands of particles packed at random** in a graphite sphere, and a
neutron has to be followed through all of them. Tutorial 5 (in progress when this page was
written) fills a sphere with particles, and meets the problem that makes Rung 5's delta tracking worth
having.
