## The question

RECONR's σ(E) assumes the target nucleus is at rest. In a reactor at 900 K the
uranium nuclei move at a few hundred metres per second, comparable to a
1 eV neutron's speed (about 14 km/s) for light nuclei and a sizeable fraction
of it near resonances for heavy ones. **What does that motion do to the
curve?**

## The shortest answer

The reaction rate depends on the **relative** speed of neutron and nucleus.
Averaging the 0 K cross section over the thermal (Maxwellian) motion of the
targets smears every resonance: **peaks fall, valleys rise, widths grow, and
the area under each resonance is kept**. This is Doppler broadening, and it is
why a hotter fuel absorbs more neutrons in its resonances when they are
self-shielded (the Doppler feedback that stabilises a reactor).

## The formula

For a free gas of targets of mass ratio `A` at temperature `T`, with neutron
speed `v` and relative speed `v_r`:

```text
σ(v, T) = (1/v²) · √(α/π) · ∫₀^∞ σ(v_r, 0) · v_r² · [ e^(−α (v − v_r)²) − e^(−α (v + v_r)²) ] dv_r

  α = A m_n / (2 k_B T)     the inverse spread of target speeds
```

NJOY's **SIGMA1** method makes this exact for a lin-lin table: in the
variable `y = √(α E)` each panel's integral reduces to differences of
`f_n(a) = (1/√π) ∫_a^∞ zⁿ e^(−z²) dz`, which are closed-form in `erfc`. The
whole derivation is in the module doc of
[`broadr/mod.rs`](https://github.com/theodoreOnzGit/outram-park-backend/blob/@@COMMIT@@/crates/njoy-outram-park-fork/src/broadr/mod.rs).
BROADR then **thins** the result: a broadened curve is smoother, so fewer
points meet the same 0.1 % tolerance (`broadn`).

## The limit that is not a formula: `thnmax`

BROADR does not broaden every energy. Above the resolved resonance range the
evaluation holds energy-**averaged** values (the unresolved range, and the
smooth range above it), and running SIGMA1 over averages is meaningless.
NJOY stops at an upper energy `thnmax`. The port of its default rule:

## Predict

U-238's resolved range ends at 20 keV. Above it the tape gives only average
resonance parameters, and BROADR leaves the range alone. **But the resonances
are still there in nature. If a reactor's neutrons stream through a slab of
U-238 at 50 keV, is the infinitely-dilute average cross section the right one
to use? Higher, lower?**
