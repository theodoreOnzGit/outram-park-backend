## The hook

> **The problem.** A cesium atom leaves the kernel easily at 1600 °C. Why
> does most of it still stay inside the particle? And if we only need the
> *fraction* that gets out, do we have to walk atoms at all?

## Step 1. How fast does an atom move in each layer? {#step-1-how-fast-does-an-atom-move-in-each-layer}

**The question.** $D$ set the size of every hop in rung 3. Where does it
come from, and why does it change so much between layers?

**The shortest answer.** Atoms jump between sites by thermal activation, so
$D$ follows an Arrhenius law in temperature, with constants measured for
each element in each material.

**The formula.** The random-walk engine uses the two-term form of [Jiang et al. (2023)](#ref-jiang2023fission)
(INL/EXT-21-63549 Rev. 1, table on p. 13):

$$D(T) = D_1\ e^{-Q_1/RT} + D_2\ e^{-Q_2/RT},$$

with $D_1, Q_1, D_2, Q_2$ per element and per layer, and, for cesium in SiC,
a fast-fluence factor on $D_1$. The second term matters only at high
temperature.

## Step 2. What happens at an interface?

**The question.** A Walk-on-Spheres hop stops exactly at the nearest
interface (rung 3). The two sides have different $D$. Does the atom cross?

**The shortest answer.** Sometimes. It crosses with a probability chosen so
that, left alone for long enough, the atoms spread out to a **uniform**
concentration: diffusion with continuous concentration and flux has no
reason to pile atoms up on either side.

**The formula.**

$$p_{\text{transmit}} = \frac{K D_2}{D_1 + K D_2},$$

for a walker on the $D_1$ side, with partition ratio $K$ ($K = 1$ means the
concentration is continuous). Why linear in $D$ and not $\sqrt{D}$? In
Walk-on-Spheres a walker re-inserted next to an interface returns to it at a
rate proportional to that side's $D$. Detailed balance,
$c_1 D_1 p_{1\to2} = c_2 D_2 p_{2\to1}$, with $c_2/c_1 = K$, gives the rule.
A fixed-time-step walk meets interfaces at a rate proportional to
$1/\sqrt{D}$ and needs a different rule; using that one here would give the
wrong equilibrium
([derivation](https://github.com/theodoreOnzGit/outram-park-backend/blob/@@COMMIT@@/crates/boon-lay/src/lagrangian_decay_simulator/lagrangian_diffusion/first_passage/interface.rs#L13-L32)).

## Step 3. Skipping the walk: the Booth equivalent sphere

**The question.** For a release *fraction*, can the diffusion equation be
solved on paper instead?

**The shortest answer.** For a sphere, yes. Booth (1957) replaced the real,
grainy, layered fuel by one uniform sphere of radius $a$ for each chemical
group. Then everything depends on $D$ and $a$ only through $D' = D/a^2$, and
the release has closed forms. This is how the
[TRISO-ATOPS port](../../api/boon_lay/triso_atops_fork/index.html) works.

**The formulas.** A long-lived species, uniform start, perfect sink: the
same Crank series as rung 3,

$$F(\tau) = 1 - \frac{6}{\pi^2}\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{e^{-n^2\pi^2\tau}}{n^2},\qquad \tau = D't.$$

A short-lived species, born continuously and decaying with $\lambda$: the
release-to-birth ratio settles to

$$\langle R/B\rangle = \frac{3}{\mu}\left(\coth\mu - \frac{1}{\mu}\right),\qquad \mu = \sqrt{\lambda a^2/D}.$$

Small $\mu$ (fast diffusion or slow decay): $\langle R/B\rangle \to 1$.
Large $\mu$: $\langle R/B\rangle \to 3/\mu$.

## Deliberate liberties on this rung

- **Elements without their own coefficients diffuse as silver** in the
  random-walk engine (step 1).
- **One temperature per layer**, no gradient.
- **$K = 1$ by default:** no chemical segregation at interfaces unless a
  caller supplies a partition ratio, and the equilibrium for $K \ne 1$ is not
  separately measured.
- **The Booth sphere** throws the layers away; TRISO-ATOPS keeps the SiC only
  for silver's breakthrough.
