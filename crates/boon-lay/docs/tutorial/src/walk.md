## The hook

A cesium atom born in the kernel jiggles from lattice site to lattice site,
billions of times a second, each jump a fraction of a nanometre. Over hours
it may wander out of the kernel.

> **The problem.** *How far does one atom get in a given time, and how do we
> simulate that without following every jump?* Then: *does a crowd of such
> atoms reproduce the release that the diffusion equation predicts?*

The demo rung for this page will be `?rung=walk` (not built yet; gh:#521).

## Step 1. How far does a jiggling atom get?

**The question.** Following $10^{9}$ jumps a second is hopeless. Is there a
shortcut?

**The shortest answer.** Add up many small random jumps and the sum is a
Gaussian, whatever the individual jumps look like (the central limit
theorem). So jump straight to the end of an interval $\Delta t$ with one
Gaussian step.

**The formula.** Each Cartesian component of the displacement is normal with
variance

$$\sigma^2 = 2D\ \Delta t,$$

where $D$ is the diffusion coefficient (m²/s). Its typical size grows as
$\sqrt{\Delta t}$, not $\Delta t$: that is the signature of diffusion.

## Step 2. Why that fails inside a TRISO particle

**The question.** The Gaussian step is exact in an *infinite* uniform
medium. A TRISO particle is a stack of thin shells with very different $D$.
What goes wrong?

**The shortest answer.** One step can be bigger than a whole layer, so an
atom can jump clean over an interface it should have met.

**The numbers** (from
[`docs/buffer_clt_failure_analysis.md`](https://github.com/theodoreOnzGit/outram-park-backend/blob/@@COMMIT@@/crates/boon-lay/docs/buffer_clt_failure_analysis.md),
arithmetic you can redo):

| $\Delta t$ | $\sigma = \sqrt{2D\Delta t}$ with $D = 10^{-8}$ m²/s | compared with |
|---|---|---|
| 1 s | 141 µm | the buffer is 100 µm thick |
| 10 s | 447 µm | the whole particle is 427.5 µm in radius |

An atom starting in the buffer can land in or beyond the SiC in one step,
never having been reflected at the buffer/IPyC interface. Shrinking
$\Delta t$ fixes it, but then an atom in the buffer needs hundreds of steps
per simulated second, while an atom in the kernel, with $D$ around
$10^{-15}$ m²/s, barely moves at all per step.

**The lesson that generalises:** the central limit theorem gives the
*free-space* answer, and a bounded, layered domain is not free space.

**The check.** This is a diagnosis, not a V&V record: the document shows the
failure mode and why the old mitigations did not cure it. The fix below is
what gets checked.

**Predict.** If the step could somehow be sized so that it *never* reaches
the nearest interface, would the time it takes still be fixed?

## Step 3. Walk-on-Spheres: hops that never cross an interface

**The question.** Can we take big steps where the atom is far from any
interface and small ones near it, *and* know exactly how long each step
took?

**The shortest answer.** Yes. Draw the largest sphere around the atom that
touches no interface. Inside it the medium is uniform, so where the atom
first leaves that sphere is uniform over its surface, and how long that
takes has a known distribution. Jump there, add that time, repeat.

**The formula.** For a walker started at the centre of a sphere of radius
$R$, the probability of still being inside at dimensionless time
$\theta = Dt/R^2$ is

$$S(\theta) = 2\sum_{k\ge1}(-1)^{k+1}e^{-k^2\pi^2\theta},\qquad \langle t\rangle = \frac{R^2}{6D}.$$

Because $\theta$ is dimensionless, its distribution is the same for every
hop of every walker. The code tabulates the inverse of $1 - S(\theta)$ once
and turns a draw into a time with $t = \theta R^2/D$. No time step appears
anywhere.

## Step 4. Does a crowd of walkers give the right release?

**The question.** The walk is built from exact pieces. Does a crowd of
walkers actually reproduce what the diffusion equation says?

**The shortest answer.** On the one case with an exact answer, yes.

**The formula.** For a sphere with a uniform initial concentration and a
perfect-sink surface (IAEA CRP-6 Case 1, a bare kernel), Crank's series
gives the fraction released by time $t$:

$$\frac{M_t}{M_\infty} = 1 - \frac{6}{\pi^2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^2}\exp\left(-\frac{Dn^2\pi^2t}{R^2}\right).$$

**The code walk.** The reference side is
[`calculate_analytical_fraction_released`](https://github.com/theodoreOnzGit/outram-park-backend/blob/@@COMMIT@@/crates/boon-lay/src/lagrangian_decay_simulator/lagrangian_diffusion/single_particle_simulator/release_fraction_analytical_solution.rs#@@L:crates/boon-lay/src/lagrangian_decay_simulator/lagrangian_diffusion/single_particle_simulator/release_fraction_analytical_solution.rs:fn=calculate_analytical_fraction_released@@)
(200 terms). The test
[`monte_carlo_test.rs`](https://github.com/theodoreOnzGit/outram-park-backend/blob/@@COMMIT@@/crates/boon-lay/src/lagrangian_decay_simulator/lagrangian_diffusion/single_particle_simulator/release_fraction_crp_6_case_1a_1b/monte_carlo_test.rs)
calls both sides with the same `D` (the Jiang correlation for Cs in UO₂, zero
fluence, [rung 4](./layers.md)).

**The check: CRP-6 Case 1, Walk-on-Spheres against Crank.** Cs-137 release
from a 212.5 µm-radius UO₂ kernel at 200 h, $N = 40\ 000$ walkers, seed
`0x0C1A5EED`. Pass if $|MC - \text{Crank}| < 0.02$; the 1σ binomial error
near $p = 0.5$ is about 0.0025. This is **verification** (against an exact
solution), not validation.

**The number to quote is the 2026-10-04 one.** Case 1a's MC value moved by
0.0001 from the July record with the same seed, so the random streams have
changed since (the commit that changed them was not traced); the shift is
well inside the 0.0025 standard deviation. The re-measurement is
recorded in the V&V record.

**A disagreement the old record hid.** The record said both analytical
values "sit in the literature-cited ranges (~0.53 at 1200 °C)". The code's
own citation says otherwise: two tests quote Hales et al. (2021, Table 4)
for Case 1a as **0.453–0.498**, and 0.5337 lies outside it. Both tests wrap
that range check in `catch_unwind`, so its failure is invisible, and assert
0.53 instead. This does not touch the walk-against-Crank verification (both
sides use the same $D$); it questions whether the crate's Case 1a inputs are
the benchmark's. The paper is not in the open corpus, so this could not be
checked here; filed as **gh:#537**, and the record's claim is struck through.

**What the check does not cover.** It is a single uniform sphere. It does
not test the interfaces (rung 4 does) and there is **no full multilayer
CRP-6 release record**: the five-layer walk is exercised only qualitatively
by `multilayer_walk_transmits_across_interfaces`.

**Predict.** Put the SiC around the kernel. Its $D$ is about a million times
smaller than in the carbon around it. What should the walk do when an atom
arrives at the SiC? That is [rung 4](./layers.md).
