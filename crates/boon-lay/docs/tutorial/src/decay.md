**Core lesson, rung 2 of 8.** The bridge from the Monte Carlo track: there, a
neutron's *flight length* is sampled from an exponential law. Here, an
atom's *lifetime* is.

## The hook

Fission in the kernel ([rung 1](./triso.md)) has just made an atom of, say,
I-131. It is radioactive. Before asking how it gets out of the particle, ask
something simpler.

> **The problem.** *When does this one atom decay, and what does it become?*
> And if we follow a million atoms one at a time, does the familiar decay law
> come out, without ever writing it down?

**Demo:** [open this rung](../../demos/triso-atops/?rung=decay). Five hundred atoms of one parent,
each with its own sampled branch and lifetimes from the crate's decay data,
coloured by what they are now, beside the surviving fraction and the decay
law it should follow.

## Step 1. When does one atom decay?

**The question.** Nobody can say when a particular I-131 atom will decay.
Every atom has the same chance per second, $\lambda$, however long it has
already existed. What does that make its lifetime?

**The shortest answer.** An exponential random number. Draw a uniform
random number $\xi$ in $(0, 1)$ and turn it into a time.

**The formula.** The chance of surviving to $t$ is $e^{-\lambda t}$. Setting
it equal to $\xi$ and solving,

$$t = -\frac{\ln \xi}{\lambda} = T_{1/2}\ \frac{|\ln \xi|}{\ln 2},\qquad \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}}.$$

This is the same trick the Monte Carlo track uses for a neutron's flight
length, $d = -\ln\xi/\Sigma_t$, with $\lambda$ in place of $\Sigma_t$.

**Statistics enters here.** A fraction counted from $N$ atoms has a

tolerance *with nothing wrong in the code*. That
is not a reason to widen the tolerance silently. It is a reason to state,
as here, what a pass means: the sampler agrees with the law to within one
standard deviation everywhere, for this seed. (Why Monte Carlo error bars
can be trusted is the Monte Carlo track's subject.)

histories, within about 5 %". **Corrected 2026-10-04**: the code draws

## Step 2. What does it become?

**The question.** Many nuclides have more than one way to decay. Bi-212
decays by β⁻ to Po-212 about 64 % of the time and by α to Tl-208 the rest.
Which one happens to *this* atom?

**The shortest answer.** Another random number, landing on a bar divided in
proportion to the branching ratios.

**The formula.** With branching ratios $b_1, b_2, \ldots$ ($\sum b_k = 1$),
pick branch $k$ when
$\sum_{j<k} b_j < \xi \le \sum_{j\le k} b_j$. The code does this by
subtracting each $b_j$ from $\xi$ until what is left fits.

```text
|--------------|----|----|
0.0          0.6   0.8   1.0
    branch 1    br 2  br 3        ξ = 0.75 lands in branch 2
```

This is the same picture as choosing a reaction from cross sections in the
Monte Carlo track.

**Show the forks.** `get_next_target_nuclide_with_float` has four exits,
and two of them are not physics:

| Case | What happens |
|---|---|
| no branches (stable) | returns `None` |
| one branch | returns that daughter |
| several branches, ξ fits | returns the chosen daughter |
| several branches, ξ is larger than the sum of the ratios | **panics** (`todo!("code is buggy!")`) |

The last case is reachable whenever an evaluation's ratios sum to slightly
less than 1, which rounding can do. A branch with **no daughter** (spontaneous
fission has none) also panics, at `decay_data.target.unwrap()`. Neither has
been seen in the crate's tests; both are filed as gh:#538
rather than hidden. Fission products are not followed here: that would need
fission yields, which [transmutation](../../deep-dives/triso-atops/transmutation.html) does not yet have.

## Step 4. One atom through a whole chain

**The question.** A time step in a simulation may be long enough for an atom
to decay several times. How does the simulator move it along?

**The shortest answer.** At birth it samples the atom's whole future: one
branch at every step (step 2) and one lifetime for every nuclide on the way
(step 1). A time step then spends lifetimes in order until it runs out.

That is allowed because decay has no memory. Sampling a nuclide's lifetime
at birth or when it is reached gives the same distribution.

## Step 5. Many atoms make the decay law

**The question.** Where is $N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ in this code?

**The shortest answer.** Nowhere. **There is no function for it.** It
*emerges* when many independent atoms are counted, which is what step 1's
check did. For a chain, the Bateman equations (the coupled decay law of
parents and daughters) emerge the same way, with no matrix:
[`count_nuclides_in_sims_linear`](https://github.com/theodoreOnzGit/outram-park-backend/blob/@@COMMIT@@/crates/boon-lay/src/lagrangian_decay_simulator/monte_carlo_single_radionuclide_decay_simulator/postprocessing.rs#@@L:crates/boon-lay/src/lagrangian_decay_simulator/monte_carlo_single_radionuclide_decay_simulator/postprocessing.rs:fn=count_nuclides_in_sims_linear@@)
just counts identities across an ensemble of simulators.

That is the point of the Lagrangian approach in this crate. A burnup code
solves $\mathrm{d}\mathbf N/\mathrm{d}t = \mathbf A\mathbf N$ with a large,
stiff matrix. Here each atom samples waiting times, and the population is a
histogram.

**The deliberate liberty.** Counting costs statistics: a nuclide present at
one atom in a million needs millions of histories to be seen at all. A
matrix method has no such floor. Which to use depends on the question.

**Predict.** The atom does not sit still while its clock runs. It diffuses.
If it decays while crossing the buffer, what changes about how it moves?
That is the [next rung](./walk.md).
