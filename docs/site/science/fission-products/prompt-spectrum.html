How fast are the prompt neutrons?

Fission products

Each fission throws out a few prompt neutrons (lesson 1). They are what keeps the chain reaction going, and in a material they are the particles that knock atoms out of place. How hard they hit depends on how fast they leave. Not all leave at the same speed: there is a spectrum of energies.

The prompt fission neutron spectrum

ENDF/B-VIII.0 MF=5/MT=18 (Brown & others, 2018), read by OUTRAM PARK’s NJOY port (MacFarlane et al., 2017). Bars: draws through outram-mc-libs’ sampler, the one its fission source uses (entry point: sample_continuous_tabular_indexed). Line: the exact distribution of that sampler, bin by bin.

Predict first. Most prompt neutrons leave with how much energy: about 0.1, 1 or 10 MeV? And is the average the same as the most common energy?

The answer, from the evaluation

Most leave with a little under 1 MeV, but a long tail of faster ones pulls the mean well above the most probable energy: two to three times it at thermal energy. Both are marked on the plot, and both are in the table below, read from the evaluated data at every incident energy it tabulates.

This evaluation gives U-235’s spectrum as a table, not a formula: the file’s law flag is … (LF = 1 is a tabulated distribution), one table of outgoing energies at each of … incident energies from …. The classic textbook shape for it is the Watt spectrum; its parameters for U-235 are source pending, so the page does not draw it.

Turn up the neutron energy

Predict first. A faster neutron brings more energy into the nucleus. Do the prompt neutrons come out faster too?

Move the slider (it is the same energy as lesson 2’s, and each lesson remembers where you left the other). The spectrum barely moves: the mean rises by a few per cent from thermal to 5 MeV, then dips twice, near 6–7 MeV and near 13–14 MeV, before rising again. Switch to Mean vs energy to see it.

Our prediction, written before the run: the mean rises slowly with incident energy, by a few per cent over the whole grid, except where a second chance of fission opens (above about 6 MeV), where neutrons emitted before the fission could pull it down; the most probable energy below 1 MeV, well below the mean. The run agrees on all four counts, including the dips. That the dips are multi-chance fission is still our hypothesis; see below.

Between the tabulated energies

At an incident energy between two tables, the Monte Carlo code does not blend the two curves point by point. It picks the upper table with probability r = (E − Ei)/(Ei+1 − Ei), linear in E, otherwise the lower one, draws an energy from it, and stretches that draw from the table’s own energy range onto a range interpolated between the two tables’ ranges (“unit-base” interpolation, as OpenMC does). The status line under the plot names the two tables and r. The evaluation’s own interpolation code for its incident grid is read and carried, but the sampler does not use it.

So a fission in the fuel sends out neutrons of about 2 MeV on average, whatever the energy of the neutron that caused it. Next question: what does a 2 MeV neutron do when it hits an atom in the crystal?

Learn more advanced stuff

Multi-chance fission, (n,n′f)

Above a few MeV the compound nucleus can first emit a neutron and then fission (second-chance fission, (n,n′f)), or emit two and then fission (third chance). Those pre-fission neutrons belong to the same fission event. This evaluation lists fission only as the total, MT=18: its file has … of the partial chances (MT=19, 20, 21, 38), so the chances cannot be shown separately here. The dips in the mean near 6–7 and 13–14 MeV are where the second and third chances would open; we predicted that softer pre-fission neutrons would lower the mean there, and it falls, but this page has not shown that this is the cause.

Where the prediction was not blind

Before the prediction above was written, lesson 1’s generator had already printed U-235’s mean at three energies (thermal, 500 keV and 14 MeV). The rest of the grid, the most probable energies and U-238 were unseen.

Same law, two codes

The sampler is a port of OpenMC’s ContinuousTabular::sample. The table at each tabulated energy is the evaluation’s own, normalised; the mean of the draws is checked against the exact mean at every slider energy, so a defect in the stretch between tables would show as a disagreement here.
