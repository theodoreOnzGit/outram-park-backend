How strong is SiC? Weakest-link statistics

Fission products

Lesson 33 gave the stress in the wall. Does the wall break? Not all at once. SiC is a ceramic, and a ceramic breaks where its worst flaw is. Every particle has different flaws, so every particle has a different strength. We cannot say whether a particle fails; we can say what fraction of them do.

The fraction that fails

boon-lay fuel failure’s weibull_failure_fraction (PANAMA-I Eq (1)), with strength and modulus after irradiation from irradiated_strength and irradiated_weibull_modulus (Eqs (8a)–(9b)). Before irradiation: 834 MPa and m = 8.02, a stand-in (the report’s HTR-Module SiC), not HTR-10 data.

Predict first. Set the stress exactly to the median strength σ0. What fraction fails? Now raise m, the “Weibull modulus”. Does the curve get steeper or flatter?

The Weibull law

φ1 = 1 − exp[ −ln 2 · (σ / σ0)m ]

  • σ0 is the median strength: at σ = σ0 exactly half the particles fail, whatever m is. The ln 2 does that. (A textbook Weibull law has no ln 2 and puts 63 % there; mixing the two shifts the strength by a few per cent without any error message, and boon-lay pins it with a test.)
  • m says how much the strength scatters. A large m means the particles are nearly alike: almost none fail below σ0, almost all above. A small m means a wide spread: some fail even at low stress.

After irradiation at 776 °C to the stand-in fluence, the code gives σ0 = … and m = …. Irradiation weakens SiC and widens its scatter (the fluence slider), down to floors of 196 MPa and m = 2 that the report sets.

Why weakest link?

A chain is as strong as its weakest link. A bigger piece of ceramic has more chances to hold a bad flaw, so it is weaker on average. A particle’s tiny SiC shell has little volume to hold one. The report’s law fixes the volume, as a particle’s; it does not scale it.
