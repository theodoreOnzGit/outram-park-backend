A tiny pressure vessel

Fission products

Not everything that leaves the kernel is held in a layer. The noble gases xenon and krypton collect in the buffer’s pores, and so does carbon monoxide made from the oxygen that fission frees in a UO2 kernel (lesson 25). The gas presses outward. The SiC shell holds it in. A TRISO particle is a pressure vessel half a millimetre across.

The model on this and the next five pages is boon-lay fuel failure. boon-lay fuel failure is inspired by PANAMA: a clean-room, agentic implementation of the equations published in the PANAMA-I report (Verfondern & Nabielek, HTA-IB-03/90). It is not a port: the PANAMA source code was not available. Every number here is computed by boon-lay fuel failure, not by PANAMA. The report is restricted literature, so only its equations and constants appear, with their numbers.

Gas, pressure and wall stress

boon-lay fuel failure’s AccidentHistory::pressure_at on the HTR-10 particle (fuel_failure::htr10::particle), irradiated at 776 °C. HTR-10 is outside the range the report claims (German TRISO, 1600–2500 °C): an extrapolation.

Predict first. Heat the particle from 1000 to 2000 °C. The ideal-gas law alone would raise the pressure by the ratio of absolute temperatures, about 1.8. Will the code’s pressure rise more or less than that?

Pressure: count the gas, use the ideal-gas law

The code counts the moles of gas in the pores and uses p = nRT / Vf, with Vf half the buffer’s volume (the report’s choice). The gas is the stable xenon and krypton that has left the kernel (0.31 atoms per fission, a fraction found by a Booth calculation like lesson 16’s), plus CO. That is PANAMA-I’s Eq (3).

At 1600 °C, 200 h into an accident, the code gives …, of which … is fission gas. The pressure rises much faster than the 1.8 of the predict prompt, because a hotter kernel also lets out more gas, and more oxygen becomes CO.

Stress: cut the sphere in half

Cut the shell through its middle. The gas pushes the halves apart with a force p · πR²; the wall holds them together with a stress σ acting on its cut ring, σ · 2πR t. Balance the two:

σ = p R / (2 t)

That is PANAMA-I’s Eq (2), for a thin SiC shell (R its mean radius, t its thickness). At 1600 °C and 200 h it gives …. A sphere halves the stress of a cylinder at the same pressure, and a small radius lowers it further.

Where it is in the code

On the Code Review map, start at AccidentHistory::pressure_at. It calls reduced_diffusion_coefficient and released_gas_fraction (how much gas has left the kernel), oxygen_per_fission (the CO), molar_volume, and then internal_gas_pressure. The stress is induced_stress_with_thinning_factor.

The derivation, and what a full model would add

The theory book derives the pressure equation term by term, settles the ambiguous fraction bar in the printed equation, tabulates the molar volumes and describes the finite-element particle that a fuel-performance code would solve: step 6, “Simplify the particle to a pressure vessel”. It is not repeated here.

Burnup

More burnup means more gas. The generator ran the same particle at burnups from 0.02 to 0.12 FIMA (failure-data.js, burnup). The prediction, written before the run, was that tripling the burnup from 0.04 to 0.12 raises the 1600 °C, 200 h pressure by more than 3 but less than 6 times.
