Steam on graphite and bare kernels

Fission products

Water can get into a gas-cooled core too, from a leaking steam generator. Steam attacks the graphite, more gently than air. And where a particle’s coatings have already failed, it reaches the kernel itself, which lets go of gas it was holding.

Two steam fits, and where they were measured

graphite_steam::specific_rate (Wang & Sun 2023) and kernel_hydrolysis::stored_gas_fraction (IAEA-TECDOC-978 Eq. 5-2), recorded on a 10 °C grid at nine steam pressures.

Steam on graphite

The reaction is C + H2O → CO + H2. Unlike burning in air, it absorbs heat, +131.3 kJ/mol, and the hydrogen it makes slows it down. The fit is a Langmuir–Hinshelwood law: steam sits on surface sites and reacts there, and hydrogen competes for the same sites.

R = k1e−E1/RT pH2Om(T) / [1 + k2e−E2/RT pH2n + k3e−E3/RT pH2Om(T)]

with m(T) a smooth step between two orders. Coefficients: Wang & Sun, Nucl. Eng. Des. 410 (2023) 112382, Table 8 (p. 11), the “Unknown n” column. The paper is cited by page, not quoted. It was measured on thin IG-110 disks at 850–1100 °C, 0.5–20 kPa of steam and up to 2 kPa of hydrogen.

At 1050 °C and 5 kPa with no hydrogen the code gives …, equal to a hand evaluation to 10−4 (the copy is checked) and inside the 10−6–10−5 /s band of the paper’s measured points at that temperature (its Fig. 17). That band is the only contact with measured data in this lesson, and a loose one. Add 1 kPa of hydrogen and the rate drops to … of that (predicted 0.45).

Steam on a bare kernel

A particle whose coatings failed (lessons 35–39) exposes its UO2 kernel. When steam reaches it, a fraction of the noble gas stored in the kernel comes out at once. A fit to the HFR-B1 and HRB-17 steam-injection tests gives that fraction (IAEA-TECDOC-978, §5.3.1.1, Eq. 5-2, p. 223):

f = 2.13×1013 P−4.353 + 6503/T e−47 257/T

with P in Pa and T in K. It was fitted at 820–1040 °C and 2.8–1051 Pa. It is a one-time burst per exposure, not a rate. Because f is a fraction of an inventory, the code clamps it to 1 and says so (ClampedToWholeInventory). At 1000 °C the fit reaches the whole inventory at … of steam (predicted 5.0 kPa), and at 770 °C at … (the TECDOC itself says “near 2 kPa”).

How far outside the fits an accident is

Now the answer to the predict prompt. At 100 or 300 kPa nothing is dotted: the graphite fit was measured up to 20 kPa and the kernel fit up to about 1 kPa, so a water-ingress accident sits two to three orders of magnitude above both. The kernel fit then returns the whole stored inventory at almost every temperature: every exposed kernel gives up all its stored noble gas. This is gh:#418, and callers must show the flag, not swallow it.

Look at the hottest temperatures at 300 kPa. The fit drops below 1 again: its pressure exponent, −4.353 + 6503/T, turns negative above 1494 K, so far outside the fit more steam releases less. That is not physics. It is what a fit does where it was never measured, and it is why the flags exist.

Not modelled at all: the slower second stage of release under continued steam (TECDOC-978 Eq. 5-3), and iodine or metal release from hydrolysed kernels.

Where it is in the code

graphite_steam::specific_rate evaluates the fit with coefficients from BlhCoefficients::wang_sun_2023_ig110 and flags InsideMeasuredRange or Extrapolated. kernel_hydrolysis::stored_gas_fraction has three exits: InsideFittedRange, Extrapolated and ClampedToWholeInventory. Both are called from outside the crate by htgr_sim_v1’s water-ingress stage (gh:#401), which reports when it extrapolates.

Checks still to do

The steam half of the kernel attack is unchecked against measured krypton numbers from Kugeler et al. (2017): 57 % of the stored Kr from two defective particles under intermittent steam at 800 °C (AVR 89/30), and cracked kernels releasing about 2 % of Kr at 5 % FIMA and more than 17 % at 9 % FIMA (gh:#444). The air half does not exist.

Why the fits are kinetic

Both graphite fits were measured where the chemistry limits (thin disks, low pressure). In a thick graphite body at high temperature, the gas diffusing into the pores limits the rate instead, so a kinetic fit extrapolated there over-states it. The air law has the oxygen supply as a guard; the steam law has none, and its flag is the only warning.
