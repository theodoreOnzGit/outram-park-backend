The R/B fingerprint

Fission products

Short-lived gas atoms decay while they diffuse. Only those born near a way out escape before decaying. The ratio of the rate released to the rate born, R/B, depends on the half-life, and how it depends tells you how the atoms got out.

R/B against decay constant

Curve: boon-lay’s booth_shortlived_fast_diffuse, D of Xe/Kr from the code. Points: Kr and Xe isotopes at their half-lives from boon-lay’s decay library. Recoil line: the RustBCA heavy-fragment range of the record (…, illustrative), from the kernel surface. Grain radius illustrative.

Predict first (we did, before running): for short half-lives, on log-log axes, the diffusion line falls with slope −½, and recoil is flat.

Two signatures

  • Diffusion: slope −½. When an atom lives only a short time, it can diffuse out only from a thin skin, about √(D/λ) deep. The released share is that skin’s volume over the sphere’s, so R/B ≈ 3√(D/λa²), falling as λ−½. This is the limit of Booth’s steady-state formula (derivation §5a: R/B → 3/μ, μ = √(λa²/D)).
  • Recoil: flat. An atom born within a fragment range of the surface, and flying outward, is out at once, whatever its half-life (lesson 20). That share does not depend on λ.

Measured R/B for several gas isotopes, plotted this way, shows which mechanism dominates: the slope is the fingerprint. check-fission.js fits the slope of boon-lay’s curve over its last two decades and finds −½ (the test prediction: within 0.01).

Knockout is flat too

Fragments passing near the surface can also knock atoms out (lesson 21). Like recoil, that does not depend on the half-life, so it adds to the flat part.
