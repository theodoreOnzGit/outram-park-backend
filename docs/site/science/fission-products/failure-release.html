From failed particles to release

Fission products

Lesson 36 gave the fraction of particles that fail. A failed particle has lost its barrier, so whatever has already left its kernel can get out. How much has left the kernel? That is the Booth sphere of chapter 3 (lessons 16 and 17), now along a changing temperature.

Failed fraction times kernel release

Kernel release: TRISO-ATOPS’s booth_transient on the time integral of D/a² (integrate_diffusion_over_time, a = the 250 µm kernel). Failed fraction: boon-lay fuel failure, lesson 36.

Predict first (we did, before running the code): at 1600 °C caesium leaves a kernel completely within 200 h, so the released share is just the failed fraction. Strontium, held by the oxide kernel (lesson 32), leaves only 5 to 15 % of it.

Release = failed × already out of the kernel

Inside a failed particle the atoms that have reached the buffer escape. Those still in the kernel keep diffusing out, at the Booth rate. So for each element

released share = φ(t) × FBooth(t)

where φ is the failed fraction and FBooth the share that has left the kernel by time t. Intact particles count as holding everything. At 1600 °C after 200 h, FBooth is … for caesium and … for strontium. Both predictions held.

The Booth release along a changing temperature uses the same series as lesson 16, with D·t replaced by the time integral of D. That is how TRISO-ATOPS handles an accident, and chapter 5 takes it apart.

Where it is in the code

Two codes meet here. boon-lay fuel failure gives φ (lesson 36). TRISO-ATOPS’s accident release integrates D over the history (integrate_diffusion_over_time) and calls booth_transient. In the workspace the bridge between them is FailureFractions::with_fuel_failure_incremental: it hands TRISO-ATOPS an in-service failure fraction computed by boon-lay fuel failure. Chapter 5, lesson 43, shows where that number goes.

What this leaves out

  • Release through intact particles (lessons 30–32): zero here. For silver at high temperature it is not zero.
  • The graphite around the particles, which holds metals back for a while (chapter 5).
  • The kernel’s own complications of chapter 3: recoil, bursts, sweeping.

Why the product is exact here

A particle that fails at time tf releases what has left its kernel by then, and afterwards whatever keeps leaving. Either way, by time t it has released the kernel’s FBooth(t). So the share released from all particles is φ(t) FBooth(t) exactly, as long as failed particles hold nothing and intact ones hold everything.
