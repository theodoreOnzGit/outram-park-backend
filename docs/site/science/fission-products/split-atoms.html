Split atoms: where do the fragments land?

Fission products

Every fission splits the compound nucleus in two, and the two pieces are a different pair almost every time. Which elements end up in the fuel depends on how often each split happens. Press 1000 fissions and watch the pairs pile up.

Fission after fission, from the evaluated yields

Yields: ENDF/B-VIII.0 independent fission yields (Brown & others, 2018), read through fission-yields-data (Ong, 2026) and summed over isomeric states. The pair drawn in each fission is this lesson’s construction; see “Learn more advanced stuff” below.

Predict first. Will the two pieces usually be the same size (two nuclei of mass number about 118), or one light and one heavy?

Two humps, not one

The products pile up in two humps: one light piece and one heavy piece. An equal split is rare; the dip between the humps is the valley. For U-235 at thermal energy the DOE handbook puts it this way: “The most probable pair of fission fragments for the thermal fission of the fuel uranium-235 have masses of about 95 and 140” (U.S. Department of Energy, 1993).

Why lopsided? Quantum shell effects: nuclei near the “magic” numbers of protons and neutrons (lesson 9 of Neutronics) are extra stable, and the split favours a heavy piece near them. Source pending: no open source with a page for this explanation is in the corpus yet.

What the curve counts

These are independent yields: each nuclide is counted after the prompt neutrons have left the fragments and before any beta decay. So the mass numbers of one pair add up to the compound nucleus’s minus the prompt neutrons; the strip above the histogram prints that sum for the last split.

Turn up the neutron energy

Predict first. Move the energy slider from thermal to 14 MeV. Does the valley fill in, stay empty, or do the humps move apart?

The valley fills in: with more energy, the split becomes more symmetric. The ratio of the peaks to the valley measures it, computed here from the evaluated curves at every energy the evaluation tabulates:

So the fragments that end up in the fuel, and therefore which elements a material has to live with, depend on what fissions and how fast the neutron was. Next question: the split also throws out neutrons. How fast are they, and does that change with the neutron energy too?

Learn more advanced stuff

Fragments or products?

Fragments are the two nuclei at scission, before they shed their prompt neutrons; products are what is left after. The independent yields (ENDF MT=454) count products, before any beta decay. Cumulative yields (MT=459) count a nuclide together with everything that decays into it, so they are larger for nuclides at the end of a decay chain. Neither is a pre-neutron fragment yield.

Only a few energies are evaluated

The evaluation tabulates the yields at only a few incident energies: the buttons under the slider. Between them, fission-yields-data interpolates every yield linearly in energy, and so does this page (it agrees with the crate’s own interpolation to the precision of the data file; check-fission.js). Linear interpolation is a choice of interpolation, not physics: nothing says the yields change in a straight line between 500 keV and 14 MeV. Because it is linear in energy, not in its logarithm, everything from thermal up to a few keV looks the same as thermal. U-238 starts at 500 keV: fission-yields-data’s thermal U-238 table is empty, and interpolating towards it would give yields that do not add up to two.

How the page draws a pair

The yields say how often each product appears, not which two appear together. Each fission here draws product 1 from Y(Z, A)/2, then its partner from the yields of the element with the remaining protons, among the isotopes light enough to leave a non-negative number of neutrons. That keeps charge and mass number, and both products then follow the evaluated curve closely but not exactly: for U-235 at thermal energy the construction’s own mass distribution, computed exactly, differs from Y(A) by at most … of the highest peak. One reason it cannot be exact: the evaluation’s yields summed by element are not perfectly charge-complementary (Y(Z) and Y(ZCN−Z) differ by up to … per fission), which a real pair set would have to be.

The symmetric mode

Fission codes often describe the mass yield as a sum of a symmetric and asymmetric modes, each a Gaussian whose weight changes with energy; CGMF’s documentation notes that the data are scarcer “in the symmetric region where fission events are rare” (Talou et al., 2024).
