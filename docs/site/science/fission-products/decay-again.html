Then it decays: the same story again

Fission products

The fragment has stopped (lessons 4 and 5). It has too many neutrons, so it is radioactive: it will decay, and its daughter may decay again, down a decay chain to a stable nucleus.

Decay sets off a few more cascades

Every decay is the same story again, with more particles. Each particle it launches starts a small cascade of its own:

  • the recoiling daughter: a knock-on of its own (lesson 5);
  • the β electron and the γ ray: they mostly ionise, which heats the fuel;
  • a delayed neutron: it flies off and makes new primary knock-ons somewhere else;
  • an alpha: a light ion that stops like a small fragment, with a heavy recoil behind it.

So one fission leaves not one cascade but a shower of cascades, spread out over years.

How the largest kicks are worked out

Momentum is conserved, so the daughter (mass M) recoils with the momentum p of what was emitted, and its energy is p²/2M. For beta decay the electron and the antineutrino share the energy Q; the daughter’s momentum is largest when the electron takes all of it, because an electron’s momentum grows faster with energy than the neutrino’s. For an alpha or a delayed neutron, two bodies share Q, and the light one takes most of the energy. The lesson generator does this (physics.rs in lesson_fission_cascades), and check-fission.js recomputes the recoils independently.

The mean decay energy is another number

The decay library also carries each nuclide’s mean energy left behind per decay (the heat, without the neutrinos). That is what decay heat uses; the recoil is the part that can move atoms.

Why the flight time is a lower bound

The range divided by the birth speed assumes the fragment never slows. It does, so it takes longer; RustBCA’s track (pending gh:#979) will give the flight time step by step. The conclusion does not hang on it: the half-lives are more than ten orders of magnitude longer.
