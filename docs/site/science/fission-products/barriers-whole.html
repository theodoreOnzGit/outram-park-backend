Across the whole particle

Fission products

Lessons 30 and 31 looked at one layer at a time. A real atom crosses all of them, and at every boundary it may bounce back. How long does it take to get out of the whole particle, and how does that change with temperature?

Atoms walking out of an HTR-10 particle

Dots and traces: this page’s layered walk, run in a Web Worker on boon-lay’s per-layer D (lesson 30). Line: the exact mean time out of the diffusion equation with the same D. One temperature through the particle; no decay; HTR-10 radii.

Predict first (we did, before running the code): the time out is set by the slowest layer. Caesium and silver are held by the SiC. Strontium, which barely moves in UO2, is held by the kernel.

The slowest layer sets the pace

Each atom walks the same way as in lesson 9. In each layer it hops at that layer’s rate; at a boundary it crosses or bounces back. Where a slow layer meets a fast one it bounces back many times before it gets in (lesson 30’s contrast).

For this simple case the diffusion equation can be solved exactly for the mean time out. Count the flux through a sphere of radius r: it must carry everything born inside, so D(r) T′(r) = −r/3, and adding up the layers from the kernel outwards gives

⟨T⟩ = a²/(15 Dkernel) + Σlayers (rout² − rin²)/(6 Dlayer)

Each layer adds its own term, and the slowest one dwarfs the rest. At 1600 °C the mean time out is … for caesium (… of it in the SiC term), … for silver, and … for strontium (… of it in the kernel term). At 1000 °C strontium would take …: the oxide kernel holds it, as Kugeler et al. (2017, p. 41) say of 1600 °C heating tests.

Compare each with the half-life (the dashed line). An atom that takes longer than its half-life to get out mostly decays inside: the particle has held it for good.

There is no multilayer diffusion solver here

The standard way to compute the full release over time, used by codes like FRESCO, solves the diffusion equation layer by layer on a mesh. The workspace does not have such a solver (theory book step 9, “FRESCO-style”). The formula above gives only the mean, for one temperature and no decay. So this page walks atoms instead.

And boon-lay’s own walker?

boon-lay has a random walker that can carry atoms through every layer: WoSWalker::walk_until_released, which calls step_multilayer over and over; at a boundary does_transmit decides whether to cross. We ran it on this particle first. It cannot get an atom out in practice. The buffer’s D is about a million times the inner PyC’s, so the walker crosses from buffer to PyC about once in a million tries, and each try costs several hops. In … steps (… of computing), a caesium atom at 1600 °C spent … of them in the buffer, got into the inner PyC … times, and never got further than … from the centre: it never reached the SiC.

The page’s walk avoids this by treating the buffer as one well-mixed shell, which its huge D makes it. It is checked against the exact mean: within 15 % at 1200–1800 °C for all three elements (check-fission.js; the prediction was written before the run).

What this walk leaves out

  • No krypton. boon-lay has no Kr coefficient in the buffer (todo!(), gh:#541). It is not walked.
  • One temperature for the whole particle, no decay, no chemistry at the boundaries (the partition ratio is 1), no fluence.
  • Intact layers only. A cracked or failed SiC is the rest of this chapter.

Learn more advanced stuff

This page’s walk

The page splits the particle into thin shells (10 in the kernel, 1 for the buffer, 4 in each other layer). An atom hops between neighbouring shells at rates from the diffusion equation in a sphere, with the D at a boundary the harmonic mean of the two sides, and each hop takes a random, exponentially distributed time. It is a teaching model, verified against the exact mean, not against a measurement.

Why the mean formula works

The mean time out, T(r), from a starting radius r obeys ∇·(D∇T) = −1, with T = 0 at the outer surface. In a sphere with concentration and flux continuous at each boundary, the flux D r²T′ at radius r equals −r³/3, the volume inside. Integrating outward and averaging over the kernel gives the formula. A different partition ratio K at a boundary would change it.
