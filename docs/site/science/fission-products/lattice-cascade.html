The lattice's side: cascade and recombination

Fission products

Near the end of its flight the fragment hits nuclei (lesson 4). Each hit hands energy to an atom of the fuel. This page is about that atom, and the ones it hits in turn.

Predict first. One fragment makes many hits. Do most of the atoms it knocks out of place stay out of place?

The primary knock-on and its cascade

The first atom the fragment strikes hard is the primary knock-on. If it gets more than the displacement energy, it leaves its site: about … in the DOE handbook’s words (U.S. Department of Energy, 1993), p. 33. NJOY’s default table, which the workspace’s NJOY port carries, gives … for uranium, … for oxygen, … for carbon and … for silicon (heatr::default_displacement_energy).

The empty site it leaves is a vacancy. Where it ends up, squeezed between sites, it is an interstitial. The pair is a Frenkel pair (U.S. Department of Energy, 1993), p. 35. If the knock-on is fast enough, it knocks on others, and they on others: a branching cascade of vacancies and interstitials (U.S. Department of Energy, 1993), p. 33.

The thermal spike, and most of it heals

The cascade dumps its energy into a tiny region, which is briefly very hot: a thermal spike (U.S. Department of Energy, 1993), p. 35. The jostling atoms find their way back: many interstitials drop into nearby vacancies and the pairs vanish (recombination). “Most of these displacements are temporary” (U.S. Department of Energy, 1993), pp. 33–34. How fast this happens (the design brief says within picoseconds) and what fraction survives in UO2: source pending.

So the answer to the prediction: no, most go back. The few that survive are the lasting damage.

Why the binary-collision code over-counts

In boon-lay’s RustBCA record of …, one light fragment’s cascade followed … recoils on average, the heavy one …. Most are not displacements: the code follows a recoil down to a cutoff of 1 to 3 eV, which the record notes “sit far below the displacement energies”. And a binary-collision code treats each collision alone, in a lattice that never relaxes, so it has no thermal spike and no recombination. That is what molecular dynamics adds (gh:#977).

Counting displacements: NRT and Kinchin–Pease

There are standard formulas that estimate how many atoms a knock-on displaces from the part of its energy that goes into nuclear collisions (the damage energy). A later lesson takes them up. Their input from nuclear data is NJOY HEATR’s damage energy, MT=444, which the workspace’s NJOY port produces (heatr::damage).

Displacement spikes

The handbook also names the displacement spike: many atoms in a small region displaced at once by a cascade, which changes the material’s properties when there are many of them (U.S. Department of Energy, 1993), p. 35.
