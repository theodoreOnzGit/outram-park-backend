Where fission products come from: one fission, step by step

Fission products · 1 of 1

A fuel crystal is not damaged by “fission” in general. It is damaged by the particular things one fission throws into it: two heavy, highly charged fragments, neutrons, gamma rays, electrons, neutrinos, and the new elements the fragments become. Each of these interacts with matter differently, so each damages the material differently. This lesson follows one fission from the neutron that starts it to the decays that follow, and names each particle as it appears.

One fission, sampled from the evaluated data

Predict first. The two fragments carry almost all the energy. Before you press Launch: will the lighter or the heavier fragment move faster? (Momentum is shared equally; kinetic energy is not.)

The question chain

  1. What does the neutron do? It joins the nucleus. U-235 plus a neutron is U-236 with extra energy: the binding energy of the neutron, Sn, plus the neutron's kinetic energy. Why that is enough to split U-236 but not U-239 is on Fission: fissile and fissionable; the levels of the compound nucleus are on Why are nuclear levels so complicated? and its ways out on What can happen in a nucleus?
  2. How does it split? The excited nucleus deforms, stretches, forms a neck and breaks into two fragments. The details are nuclear physics we point to, not derive. What matters here is which pair comes out: the demo draws it from the evaluated independent fission yields (ENDF/B-VIII.0, through fission-yields-data), keeping charge and mass number:

    Z1 + Z2 = ZCN,   A1 + A2 + ν = ACN

  3. Where does the energy come from? From mass. The split's Q-value is the mass that disappears:

    Q = M(CN*) − M1 − M2 − ν mn,    M(CN*) = M(CN) + E*,   E* = Sn + Ecm

    with the masses from the NuDat table (National Nuclear Data Center, 2026). Most of Q becomes the fragments' kinetic energy: the evaluation's average, EFR, is 169 MeV for U-235 at thermal energy (MF=1/MT=458, Brown & others, 2018). The rest is excitation energy in the fragments.
  4. How fast do they fly? Momentum is conserved. In the frame of the compound nucleus the fragments fly back to back with equal and opposite momenta; the incoming neutron's tiny momentum then shifts both. The demo does this exactly, relativistically, in two dimensions, and prints the residual: it is round-off.
  5. Why do they shed neutrons? Look at the readout's N/Z. The fragments keep the uranium's ratio of neutrons to protons, about 1.56, but a stable nucleus of their mass has fewer neutrons per proton. They are neutron-rich: they boil off neutrons at once (the prompt neutrons), then convert neutrons to protons by beta decay for seconds to years. Some of those beta decays leave a nucleus that emits a neutron: the delayed neutrons.

The same event in numbers

Yields: ENDF/B-VIII.0 independent yields, summed over Z. N/Z: the stable line is the isobar of lowest mass in the NuDat table. Energy budget: MF=1/MT=458. Spectra: MF=5/MT=18 (prompt) and MF=5/MT=455 (the six delayed groups), read by OUTRAM PARK's NJOY port (MacFarlane et al., 2017).

What hits the lattice

From the point of view of the fuel crystal and the material around it, one fission is a small particle shower: one event branches into many particles, and those go on to make more. A neutron hits another nucleus; a fragment beta-decays and emits a gamma ray, which knocks out an electron; and so on. Energy shares are U-235 at thermal energy, from MT=458.

What the checks say

The page's physics is checked by check-fission.js under node, against the data and against itself. Measured 2026-10-20:

  • Conservation, 2000 events each case: worst |Σp − pn| about 10−11 MeV/c and worst |ΔE| about 10−10 MeV out of 2.2×105 MeV, i.e. round-off. Ternary events the same.
  • The yields agree with ν̄. Mass balance on the yields alone gives ν = ACN − ΣY·A = 2.400, against the evaluation's prompt ν̄p = 2.414 (MT=456).
  • A miss, shown as it is. The demo's pair construction gives a mean of 2.06 prompt neutrons per fission, about 15 % short of both. The independent yields carry no correlation between the two fragments, so the partner is drawn from its element's yields regardless of the first fragment; that construction is the likely cause. The fix needs pair-correlated yields, which this lesson does not have.
  • Delayed neutrons. Following every product's NuDat decay chain gives 0.022 delayed neutrons per fission against the evaluation's ν̄d = 0.01585 (MT=455): the two data sets disagree by about 40 %, and the page does not hide it.

So many particles, so many ways to damage the material

One fission puts into the lattice two heavy charged fragments that ionise and stop within it, neutrons that knock atoms and make new nuclei, gamma rays and electrons that ionise, neutrinos that leave, and some forty new elements that stay. Each is a different source of damage or activation, and later lessons take them one at a time.

Fragments or products?

This page calls the two nuclei fragments from scission until their prompt neutrons have left, and products after. The independent yields (ENDF MT=454) count products: after the prompt neutrons, before any beta decay. A pre-neutron fragment yield is a different quantity, and the evaluation does not give it. Cumulative yields (MT=459) count a nuclide after everything that decays into it.

How the demo builds one event (its deliberate liberties)

  1. The pair. Fragment 1 is drawn from the independent yields; its partner from the yields of the complementary element, with A2 ≤ ACN − A1; ν is what is left. A pair that cannot pay for its neutrons is not drawn. It is a construction, and it gives too few neutrons (above).
  2. TKE is the evaluated average EFR(E) for every event; the spread from split to split is not modelled. EFR falls with incident energy in the evaluation (169.13 MeV at thermal, 165.26 MeV at 30 MeV for U-235). Madland's systematics, as quoted in CGMF's documentation, give TKEpost = (169.13 ± 0.07) − 0.2660 En MeV and TKEpre = (170.93 ± 0.07) − 0.1544 En MeV (Talou et al., 2024); Madland's paper itself was not read. That EFR equals TKEpost at thermal energy suggests ENDF's EFR is the post-neutron value: not verified against ENDF-102.
  3. The excitation is shared between the fragments in proportion to their mass numbers, an assumption (equal temperatures). CGMF instead uses a ratio of temperatures RT that is calibrated, “adjusted to fit” data; it is not used here.
  4. Prompt neutron energies in each fragment's frame are drawn from ε exp(−ε/T), with T chosen so that the mean laboratory energy is the evaluation's ENP/ν̄p; emission is isotropic in the fragment's frame (in the plane of the picture), and each fragment recoils.
  5. Prompt gammas: what is left of each fragment's excitation leaves as one photon. Real cascades have several, and isomeric levels live “from nanoseconds to seconds and even longer”: in U-235 thermal fission, Te-134's 6+ level at 1.691 MeV has a half-life of 164 ns. CGMF stamps prompt neutrons and continuum gammas at time 0 (Talou et al., 2024, isomers and timing documentation). When the cascade starts is source pending.
  6. Masses are the NuDat table's, rounded there to 3 significant figures (up to ±50 keV each), so Q is good to roughly 0.1 MeV; Sn from the table is 6.57 MeV against the evaluation's capture Q of 6.544 MeV. The energy is still conserved exactly, because every mass in an event comes from the same table.
  7. Delayed neutrons: a precursor's NuDat beta-delayed-neutron branching decides whether one is emitted, and its energy is drawn from the MF=5/MT=455 group whose decay constant is nearest the precursor's ln 2/t½. The evaluation's six groups are a fit over many precursors; matching one precursor to one group is this lesson's rule.

Ternary fission

Now and then a light charged particle, usually an alpha, leaves from the neck at scission. The demo can make one (the panel's switch, or ?ternary=1): three-body momentum conservation, alpha perpendicular to the fission axis. How often it happens is source pending: the ENDF/B-VIII.0 fission yield library does not include the light particle, and the zeros fission-yields-data returns for H-3 and He-4 are fall-through defaults, not evaluated values. The alpha's energy is a placeholder rule (EFR × 4/ACN), labelled as such on screen.

Delayed-neutron precursors

The evaluation lumps the precursors into six groups by decay constant (MF=1/MT=455) and gives each group its own energy spectrum (MF=5/MT=455, a histogram). The spectra chart shows how much softer the delayed neutrons are than the prompt ones.
