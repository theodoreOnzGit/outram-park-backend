Where fission products come from: one fission, step by step

Fission products

This track is about the materials around a fission: what damages them and what makes them radioactive. How a nucleus splits is nuclear physics the track takes as given; what matters here is what comes out, and why each piece does what it does. A fuel crystal is not damaged by “fission” in general. It is damaged by the particular things one fission throws into it: two heavy, highly charged fragments, neutrons, gamma rays, electrons, neutrinos, and the new elements the fragments become. Each of these interacts with matter differently, so each damages the material differently. This lesson follows one fission from the neutron that starts it to the decays that follow, and names each particle as it appears.

One fission, sampled from the evaluated data

Predict first. The two fragments carry almost all the energy. Before you press Launch: will the lighter or the heavier fragment move faster? (Momentum is shared equally; kinetic energy is not.)

The question chain

  1. What does the neutron do? It joins the nucleus. U-235 plus a neutron is U-236 with extra energy: the binding energy of the neutron, Sn, plus the neutron's kinetic energy. Why that is enough to split U-236 but not U-239 is on Fission: fissile and fissionable; the levels of the compound nucleus are on Why are nuclear levels so complicated? and its ways out on What can happen in a nucleus?
  2. How does it split? The excited nucleus wobbles and breaks into two fragments. How, in detail, is quantum and nuclear physics this track does not derive. What matters here is which pair comes out: the demo draws it from the evaluated independent fission yields (ENDF/B-VIII.0, through Ong, 2026), keeping charge and mass number:

    Z1 + Z2 = ZCN,   A1 + A2 + ν = ACN

  3. Where does the energy come from? From mass. The split's Q-value is the mass that disappears:

    Q = M(CN*) − M1 − M2 − ν mn,    M(CN*) = M(CN) + E*,   E* = Sn + Ecm

    with the masses from the NuDat table (National Nuclear Data Center, 2026). Most of Q becomes the fragments' kinetic energy: the evaluation's average, EFR, is … for U-235 at thermal energy (MF=1/MT=458, Brown & others, 2018). The rest is excitation energy in the fragments.
  4. How fast do they fly? Momentum is conserved. In the frame of the compound nucleus the fragments fly back to back with equal and opposite momenta; the incoming neutron's tiny momentum then shifts both. The demo does this exactly, relativistically, in two dimensions, and prints the residual: it is round-off.
  5. Why do they shed neutrons? Look at the readout's N/Z. The fragments keep the compound nucleus's ratio of neutrons to protons, … for U-236, but a stable nucleus of their mass has fewer neutrons per proton (the N/Z chart below). They are neutron-rich: they boil off neutrons at once (the prompt neutrons), then turn neutrons into protons by beta decay, each at its own half-life. Some of those beta decays leave a nucleus that emits a neutron: the delayed neutrons.

The same event in numbers

Yields: ENDF/B-VIII.0 independent yields, summed over Z. N/Z: the stable line is the isobar of lowest mass in the NuDat table. Energy budget: MF=1/MT=458. Spectra: MF=5/MT=18 (prompt) and MF=5/MT=455 (the six delayed groups), read by OUTRAM PARK's NJOY port (MacFarlane et al., 2017).

What hits the lattice

From the point of view of the fuel crystal and the material around it, one fission is a small particle shower: one event branches into many particles, and those go on to make more. A neutron hits another nucleus; a fragment beta-decays and emits a gamma ray, which knocks out an electron; and so on. Energy shares are U-235's at thermal energy, from MT=458; ET is the evaluation's total.

What the checks say

The page's physics is checked by check-fission.js under node, against the data and against itself; the numbers below are computed by the page from the data.

  • Conservation. Every event conserves energy and momentum to the computer's round-off; the check gates the worst residual over 2000 events per case at 10−9 MeV/c and 10−8 MeV, ternary events included.
  • The yields agree with ν̄. Mass balance on the yields alone gives ν = ACN − ΣY·A = … for U-235 at thermal energy, against the evaluation's prompt ν̄p = … (MT=456): two independent parts of ENDF/B-VIII.0. At 14 MeV the mass balance falls below ν̄p, the sign expected when some neutrons leave before the nucleus splits (lesson 3); for U-238 at 500 keV it lies above, and that is unexplained.
  • A miss, shown as it is. The demo's pair construction gives a mean of … prompt neutrons per fission, … short of ν̄p. The independent yields carry no correlation between the two fragments, so the partner is drawn from its element's yields whatever the first fragment was; that gives far too wide a spread of ν, and the energy test then removes the pairs with most neutrons. Pair-correlated yields would fix it; this lesson does not have them.
  • Delayed neutrons. Following every product's NuDat decay chain gives … delayed neutrons per fission against the evaluation's ν̄d = … (MT=455): the two data sets disagree by …, and the page does not hide it.

So many particles, so many ways to damage the material

One fission puts into the lattice two heavy charged fragments that ionise and stop within it, neutrons that knock atoms and make new nuclei, gamma rays and electrons that ionise, neutrinos that leave, and new elements that stay. Each is a different source of damage or activation, and later lessons take them one at a time.

Fragments or products?

This page calls the two nuclei fragments from scission until their prompt neutrons have left, and products after. The independent yields (ENDF MT=454) count products: after the prompt neutrons, before any beta decay. A pre-neutron fragment yield is a different quantity, and the evaluation does not give it. Cumulative yields (MT=459) count a nuclide after everything that decays into it.

How the demo builds one event (its deliberate liberties)

  1. The pair. Fragment 1 is drawn from the independent yields; its partner from the yields of the complementary element, with A2 ≤ ACN − A1; ν is what is left. A pair that cannot pay for its neutrons is not drawn. It is a construction, and it gives too few neutrons (above).
  2. TKE is the evaluated average EFR(E) for every event; the spread from split to split is not modelled. EFR falls with incident energy in the evaluation: for U-235, … at thermal energy and … at …, the top of its table. Madland's systematics, as quoted in CGMF's documentation, give TKEpost = (169.13 ± 0.07) − 0.2660 En MeV and TKEpre = (170.93 ± 0.07) − 0.1544 En MeV (Talou et al., 2024); Madland's paper itself was not read. That EFR equals TKEpost at thermal energy suggests ENDF's EFR is the post-neutron value: not verified against ENDF-102.
  3. The excitation is shared between the fragments in proportion to their mass numbers, an assumption (equal temperatures). CGMF instead shares it through a ratio of temperatures RT, which its documentation calls “a free parameter, which can be tuned to be best reproduce the average prompt fission neutron multiplicity as a function of the fragment mass” (Talou et al., 2024): calibrated, so it is not used here.
  4. Prompt neutron energies in each fragment's frame are drawn from ε exp(−ε/T), with T chosen so that the mean laboratory energy is the evaluation's ENP/ν̄p; emission is isotropic in the fragment's frame (in the plane of the picture), and each fragment recoils.
  5. Prompt gammas: what is left of each fragment's excitation leaves as one photon. Real cascades have several, and isomeric levels live “from nanoseconds to seconds and even longer”: in U-235 thermal fission, Te-134's 6+ level at 1.691 MeV has a half-life of 164 ns. (Talou et al., 2024). CGMF also assumes that “the neutron emission is assumed to happen only once both fragments are fully accelerated” (fission yields documentation): a modelling choice, not a measured time. When the cascade starts is source pending.
  6. Masses are the NuDat table's, rounded there to 3 significant figures (up to ±50 keV each), so Q is good to roughly 0.1 MeV; Sn of U-236 from the table is … against the evaluation's capture Q of … (MF=3/MT=102). The energy is still conserved exactly, because every mass in an event comes from the same table.
  7. Delayed neutrons: a precursor's NuDat beta-delayed-neutron branching decides whether one is emitted, and its energy is drawn from the MF=5/MT=455 group whose decay constant is nearest the precursor's ln 2/t½. The evaluation's six groups are a fit over many precursors; matching one precursor to one group is this lesson's rule.

Ternary fission

Now and then a light charged particle, usually an alpha, leaves from the neck at scission. The demo's ternary events (and every event, with the panel's switch or ?ternary=1) conserve momentum among three bodies, with the alpha perpendicular to the fission axis. How often it happens is source pending. ENDF/B-VIII.0's fission-yield library carries no H-3 or He-4 for Pu-239 (checked), and nothing found for U-235 contradicts that; the zeros fission-yields-data returns for H-3 and He-4 are fall-through defaults, not evaluated values, so this page shows none of them as data. The demo makes every tenth launch ternary so that a reader meets one: a demo choice, never a rate. The alpha's energy is a placeholder rule (EFR × 4/ACN), labelled as such on screen.

Delayed-neutron precursors

The evaluation lumps the precursors into six groups by decay constant (MF=1/MT=455) and gives each group its own energy spectrum (MF=5/MT=455, a histogram on U-235's tape). The spectra chart shows how much softer the delayed neutrons are than the prompt ones.
