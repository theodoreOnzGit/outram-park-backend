What happens, hour by hour

Play the heat-up to 1800 °C. The burst fraction φ1 climbs while the temperature climbs and stops when it cools: a particle that has burst does not un-burst. Decomposition φ2 stays tiny unless the peak passes about 2000 °C. The total combines them, and an as-manufactured defect fraction φ0 if you add one, by multiplying survival chances: a particle survives only if it survives every route.

φ = 1 − (1 − φ0)(1 − φ1)(1 − φ2)

Hold 200 h at a fixed temperature (the hold options) and the code gives φ1 = … at 1200 °C, … at 1600 °C and … at 2000 °C. At 2200 °C decomposition takes over: φ2 = … against φ1 = …. Learn more advanced stuff

Not a normal-operation failure fraction

At the end of irradiation the model gives a burst fraction of about 10−12. That is not the fraction of particles that fail in service. In-service failures come from manufacturing defects, which the model takes as an input (φ0; the report suggests 6×10−5 as a target, which boon-lay keeps as a named constant, never a default). The theory book discusses what this means for the HTR-10 digital twin: step 7, and the deep dive HTR-10: an extrapolation.

Step size

At constant temperature the answer does not depend on how the time is cut into steps; boon-lay tests that to 2×10−12. The 5 h steps here are for drawing.
