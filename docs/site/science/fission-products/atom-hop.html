How an atom hops

Fission products

Lesson 7 said atoms diffuse, and faster when hot. Here is what one atom is actually doing.

One atom in its site

A kinetic Monte Carlo of an atom on a square lattice. The barrier is illustrative (you set it). Time is counted in rattles, so no vibration frequency is assumed. The cloud and the Arrhenius plot are computed in a Web Worker.

Predict first. Raise the temperature from 1000 K to 1500 K, with a 1 eV barrier. Does the atom hop half again as often, twice as often, or dozens of times as often?

It rattles, and now and then it squeezes through

  • The atom sits in its site, held by its neighbours, and rattles (vibrates) all the time. The hotter the crystal, the harder it rattles.
  • To move to the next site it must squeeze between its neighbours. That costs energy: a hill between the two sites, the migration barrier Em.
  • Most rattles are far too weak. Now and then one is big enough to carry it over the hill, into the next site. That is one hop.

The chance that one rattle is big enough falls off exponentially with the height of the hill compared with the typical thermal energy kT. So the hop rate is

Γ = ν exp(−Em / kT)

where ν is how often it rattles (the attempt frequency; its size: source pending) and exp(−Em/kT) is the chance that one rattle succeeds. This is the Arrhenius law for one hop (Tian group, University of Alberta, 2026), Lecture 10, “Arrhenius jump frequency”.

Hotter means exponentially more hops

kT is small: … at 1000 K and … at 1500 K (computed on the page from the Boltzmann constant). A barrier of 1 eV is many times kT, so the chance per rattle is tiny, and a small change in T changes it a lot. Move the slider and watch the counter “1 in …”: for a 1 eV barrier, going from 1000 K to 1500 K makes it hop … as often. Not half again, not twice: dozens of times, from a 50 % rise in temperature.

Many hops make diffusion

Each hop goes to a random neighbour. After many hops the atom has done a random walk. Release a cloud of atoms in the demo (“A cloud”): it spreads into a round, bell-shaped blob, and its width grows as the square root of time. That spreading is diffusion, and its speed is set by the hop rate. So diffusion follows the Arrhenius law too.

The Arrhenius plot view (in the panel) runs the hops at eight temperatures and plots ln D against 1/T. The points fall on a straight line, and its slope gives back the barrier you set.

Learn more advanced stuff

Why exp(−E/kT): counting states

Think of the atom as a small system in contact with the crystal around it, a big reservoir at temperature T. Every arrangement (microstate) of the whole is equally likely. For the atom to hold an extra energy E, the reservoir must give it up, and a reservoir with less energy has fewer arrangements: its entropy drops by about E/T, so its number of arrangements drops by a factor exp(−E/kT). Fewer arrangements, lower probability: that is the Boltzmann factor. OpenStax states Boltzmann’s result, with the “negative exponential” of the total mechanical energy, in (Ling et al., 2016), §2.4, p. 91, and the statistical view of entropy in §4.7, pp. 154–155.

Why a Gaussian: the central limit theorem

The atom’s position after n hops is a sum of n independent random steps. By the central limit theorem, such a sum tends to a Gaussian whose variance grows in proportion to n, so in proportion to time: ⟨R²⟩ = 2dDt in d dimensions (6Dt in 3D, 4Dt on the demo’s flat lattice). With Γtot the total hop rate and λ the hop length, D = Γtotλ²/(2d): (1/6)Γtotλ² in 3D. In the continuum limit this is Fick’s law. It is the same pair of ideas as the Monte Carlo lessons: sample one step at a time, then let the central limit theorem tell you the spread. boon-lay’s Lagrangian walker uses exactly that (its central_limit_theorem module).

The fit, checked

Before running it, we wrote down that the fitted slope would give back a 1.00 eV barrier to within 3 % (1000 atoms, about 400 hops each, 8 temperatures from 900 to 1950 K). check-fission.js runs the same Monte Carlo under node and fails the site build if it does not; on 2026-10-20 it gave 1.0000 eV, r² = 0.99959.

The chemists’ form

Chemists write the same law for a rate constant, k = A exp(−Ea/RT), and read the activation energy off the slope of ln k against 1/T (Flowers et al., 2019), §12.5, p. 612. R = NAk per mole; k per atom.
