Why SiC matters most, and silver gets through

Carbon stops gases, not metals

Wichner (1991, NUREG/CR-5647, p. 13) puts it in one sentence: “Diffusivities of metallic FPs through the PyC coatings, which completely stop the fission gases, are sufficiently high so that an intact SiC coating is required to prevent diffusive release of the metals from fuel particles.” That is the BISO story of lesson 29, and the reason TRISO adds SiC. The matrix shows it: the PyC rows hold krypton up to about 1200 °C, but leak caesium and strontium well below that.

SiC holds metals, except silver

In boon-lay’s coefficients the SiC is the slowest solid layer for every metal. At 1200 °C, caesium moves … times faster in PyC than in SiC. Kugeler et al. (2017, Table 5, p. 33) say the SiC “retains all gaseous and solid fission products (except Ag) at normal operating temperatures < 1 250 °C and in accidents up to 1 600 °C”. Wichner (p. 13) reports that “intact Triso particles retain Cs and Sr to temperatures well above that expected in normal operation, but that silver is readily released above ~1423 K (1150°C).” Watch the Ag column go amber, then red.

One surprise from the code: strontium is held best by the kernel itself. Kugeler et al. (p. 41) say the same: “Only the fission product 90Sr is strongly retained in the oxide kernels at this temperature” (1600 °C). Lesson 32 shows what that does to the release.

No magic material

There is no one material that holds back everything. Carbon holds the gases but leaks metals; SiC holds the metals but has its own weaknesses: silver gets through, palladium and the lanthanides attack it, and it decomposes at very high temperature (lesson 35). So we use several layers that cover each other’s weaknesses. That is defence in depth again (lesson 29), now inside a single particle.

Where it is in the code

The PyC-to-SiC contrast is not a million

The crate once said SiC’s D is “about 106 times smaller” than the pyrocarbon’s. With the activation energies included, the contrast is 13 to 4100 over Cs, Sr, Ag and Kr at 1000–1600 °C, and for Ag and Kr it shrinks as the temperature rises. The theory book records the correction and the transmission probability at the interface: step 4, “Silicon carbide”. It also names the sources for palladium and lanthanide attack, which are not in the open corpus.

Why silver?

How silver gets through intact SiC is not settled in the sources the workspace has; the theory book’s step 3 quotes the open question. Wichner (p. 13) adds that silver’s release through SiC “is somewhat unpredictable, perhaps dependent on SiC coating quality” (citing Nabielek et al. 1977).
