The diffusion equation, three ways

Fission products

Chapters 2 to 4 met diffusion three times: atoms hopping (Atoms swapping), walkers on spheres (Walking on spheres) and Booth’s formula (Booth on one grain). Before TRISO-ATOPS is taken apart, this lesson puts them side by side: one equation, solved exactly, on a mesh and by walkers. The next lesson then asks whether TRISO-ATOPS’s exact formulas are the safe side of the whole particle.

The equation

Two statements make it. Fick’s law: atoms flow down the concentration gradient, J = −D ∇c, with D the diffusion coefficient (m²/s) that the hop lessons built from single jumps. Conservation: the atoms in a small volume change only by what flows across its surface, what is born in it and what decays, ∂c/∂t = −∇·J + s − λc. Together, in a sphere where nothing depends on direction:

∂c/∂t = (1/r²) ∂/∂r (r² D ∂c/∂r) − λc + s

c is atoms per m³, λ the decay constant (1/s), s the birth rate per m³ per s. The r² is geometry: the shell at radius r has area 4πr², so the same flux spreads thinner further out.

An equation alone is not a problem. It needs a start and edges. The case all three methods below solve is CRP-6’s Case 1a: caesium spread evenly through a 212.5 µm kernel (c = c0 at t = 0), no birth, no decay, constant D = … at 1200 °C, and a perfect sink at the surface (c = 0 at r = a: whatever arrives is gone). The centre is a symmetry point, so no flux crosses it.

Predict first. A mesh of only five cells across the kernel: how far off do you expect its released fraction to be at 200 h, when Crank’s answer is about one half? One per cent? Ten?

Way 1: exactly, by separating variables

Try a solution that is a shape times a decay, c = R(r) e−κt. The equation splits into one for the shape, whose solutions that vanish at the surface and stay finite at the centre are

Rn(r) = sin(nπr/a)/r,   κn = n²π²D/a²,  n = 1, 2, 3, …

Each is a mode: a fixed shape that fades at its own rate, the n-th n² times faster than the first. Adding them up to match the flat start gives the concentration (Crank 1975, eq. 6.18):

c/c0 = (2a/πr) Σn≥1 ((−1)n+1/n) sin(nπr/a) exp(−n²π²Dt/a²)

and, counting what has crossed the surface, the fraction released:

F(t) = 1 − (6/π²) Σn≥1 (1/n²) exp(−n²π²Dt/a²)

That is Booth’s formula, the one in Booth on one grain. Two things to notice. Time enters only as Dt/a²: double D or halve the time and the answer is the same. And the modes are inside the derivation: the formula for F keeps only their sum at the surface, so a code that evaluates it, as TRISO-ATOPS does, never stores a concentration field at all.

Way 2: on a mesh

The exact route needs a uniform sphere. A real particle has five layers with D varying over twelve orders of magnitude, and the exact route stops there. A mesh does not care. Cut the sphere into thin shells (cells); in each, keep one number, the mean concentration. Between two neighbours the flow is a conductance times their difference (Patankar, 1980), where the conductance is the face area over the series resistance of the two half cells, G = A / (Δr1/2D1 + Δr2/2D2), exact even where D jumps at a layer boundary. Then step in time:

Vi (cinew − ci)/Δt = −Σj Gij (cinew − cjnew) − λVicinew

Using the new values on the right (backward Euler) makes every step a small linear system, tridiagonal because each cell only talks to two neighbours, and stable for any Δt. The atoms leaving through the surface are counted from the same values, so atoms are conserved to round-off.

Way 3: by walkers

Follow atoms instead of concentrations. Each atom does a random walk whose spread grows as 6Dt; the fraction of walkers that have reached the surface by time t is the released fraction. Walking on spheres does this with hops sized to the nearest boundary, and its 10 000 walkers sit on Crank’s curve (Crank, 1975) within their binomial error. The walk solves the same equation, read backwards: the concentration is a density of walkers.

Which to use when

  • Exact series: one uniform sphere (or slab), constant or time-rescaled D, a simple start. Cheap, exact, but the geometry is fixed. TRISO-ATOPS lives here.
  • Mesh: any number of layers, any D(r, t), births and decay, the profile itself. Its error is measurable and shrinks as the cells shrink. FRESCO-type codes live here.
  • Walkers: any geometry, and atoms carry their own history; but the answer has statistical noise, and very slow layers make walks very long (Across the whole particle).

Where it is in the code

  • The exact release: booth_longlived (long-lived), booth_transient (the same series on ∫D dt), ported from TRISO-ATOPS.
  • The mesh: LayeredSphere::new builds cells per layer; LayeredDiffusion::step takes one backward-Euler step with one D per layer, solved by petir’s port of GSL’s tridiagonal solver. It is boon-lay’s own code, not a port (gh:#1209), kept apart from the TRISO-ATOPS fork.
  • The walkers: mc_kernel_release_fraction.

How the mesh was checked

Verification, not validation: each check compares the mesh with an exact solution of the same equation. Written down before the first run (multilayer_diffusion.rs, P1–P4), results the same day:

  • One sphere against Crank’s series: within 7.9×10−5, 3.0×10−5 and 1.1×10−5 at Dt/a² = 0.01, 0.05, 0.2 (200 cells).
  • Five layers with D spread over seven orders of magnitude: the mean time to leave matches the exact layered formula of Across the whole particle to 1.4×10−4.
  • A decaying species born at a steady rate: the release-to-birth ratio matches Booth’s exact R/B to 6.8×10−7 and 6.8×10−4 (the R/B lesson).
  • Atoms conserved to 6.6×10−12 over 200 stiff steps. The prediction, 10−12, missed: the solve’s round-off grows with the buffer’s DΔt/Δr² ≈ 1100, which the prediction ignored. The test now gates on that derived bound.

Learn more advanced stuff

Why time enters only as ∫D dt

If D changes with time but not with position (one temperature for the whole sphere), define a new clock θ = ∫0t D(t′) dt′. Then ∂c/∂θ = ∇²c, with no D left: the solution is Crank’s with Dt replaced by θ. That is why TRISO-ATOPS can follow a whole accident with one integral and one formula, and why it is exact only when there is one D for the sphere, no source and no decay during the transient.

Why the mesh conserves atoms exactly

Sum the step equation over all cells: every inner flow appears twice with opposite signs and cancels, leaving the change in atoms = −(flow out of the surface + decay) × Δt, all from the new values. The code adds exactly those two terms to its running totals, so inside + released + decayed is 1 up to the linear solve’s round-off.
