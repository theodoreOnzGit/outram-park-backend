Many Booth spheres

Fission products

A kernel is a packing of grains. Each is a little Booth sphere, with its own size and its own temperature, so its own D. The kernel releases the sum, weighted by how many atoms each grain holds.

One sphere or many

Each grain uses its local D from the code’s class for your family. Grain-size spread: illustrative (a lognormal of chosen width). Temperatures: from the kernel profile below.

Predict first. Which matters more in a TRISO kernel: the spread of grain sizes, or the temperature difference between the kernel’s centre and its edge?

The temperature inside a kernel, worked out

HTR-10’s core-average power per particle is …: its thermal power divided by its fuel pebbles and the particles in each, from the workspace’s cited HTR-10 data. Spread over the kernel that is …. Conducting it out through UO2 (tampines’ fresh-fuel conductivity) makes the centre only … hotter than the surface. tampines’ own particle solver agrees to within hundredths of a kelvin.

So, the answer to the prediction: in a TRISO kernel the size spread matters, and the radial temperature hardly does. Toggle both and see.

Why the shortcut can miss

Booth’s release is not a straight line in D/a²: early on it grows as its square root, later it saturates at 1. So the release of the average grain is not the average release of the grains. Small grains empty first and dominate early; big grains hold out late. One “equivalent” sphere can match one moment and miss the rest.

The existing analogue

TRISO-ATOPS evaluates Booth per fuel node, each node at its own temperature, and sums over nodes (normal_operation_node); this page does the same one level down, per grain.

What the temperature profile assumes

Fresh fuel (tampines’ conductivity has no burnup term; lesson 26), uniform power density across the kernel, and the HTR-10 core average. A hot spot in the core would raise every temperature here, which the panel’s shift slider imitates.
