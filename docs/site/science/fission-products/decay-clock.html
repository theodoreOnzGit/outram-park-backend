An atom’s clock: sampling a decay

Fission products

Lesson 6 followed one fragment down its decay chain. But nobody can say when a particular atom of I-131 will decay, or, for an atom with a choice, what it will become. So how does a computer give it a history? And if it gives a million atoms one history each, does the familiar decay law come out, without anyone writing it down?

Predict first. Pick Kr-88 and then Cs-137: their half-lives differ by a factor of about 90 000. On the plot, whose axis is time in half-lives, does the shape of the parent’s curve change?

When does one atom decay?

Every atom of a nuclide has the same chance per second of decaying, λ, however long it has already existed. That makes its lifetime an exponential random number. The chance of surviving to t is e−λt; set it equal to a uniform random number ξ between 0 and 1, and solve:

t = −ln ξ / λ = T½ |ln ξ| / ln 2,   λ = ln 2 / T½

It is the same trick the Monte Carlo track uses for how far a neutron flies before it collides (How far to the next collision?), with λ in place of Σt. Measured in half-lives, the lifetime is |ln ξ|/ln 2 whatever the nuclide: so the answer to the prompt is no, only the time axis changes.

What does it become?

Many nuclides can decay in more than one way. A second random number picks which: lay the branching ratios end to end on a bar from 0 to 1, and the slot ξ lands in is the branch.

The bar of branching ratios

Ratios: boon-lay’s decay library. Sampled shares: the first daughters of the … atoms of each parent.

Bi-212 decays by beta to Po-212 … of the time and by alpha to Tl-208 …; of the sampled atoms, … went to Tl-208. Cs-137 goes to the metastable Ba-137m … of the time (sampled: …), and Ba-137m, a few minutes later, gives off the 662 keV gamma ray Cs-137 is known for. Every sampled share is within 4 binomial standard deviations of its ratio (check-decaywalk.js).

Two forks that are not physics

The routine that walks the bar has four exits: no branch (stable), one branch, the slot found, and ξ landing past the end of the bar. That last one panics; it can happen when an evaluation’s ratios add up to slightly less than 1. A branch with no daughter (spontaneous fission) panics too. Both are filed as gh:#538. For the five parents here the ratios add up to 1 within 10−6 (checked). Fission products of a fission branch are not followed: that would need fission yields.

Where the half-lives come from

From the ENDF/B-VIII.0 evaluation (Brown & others, 2018), as OpenMC’s depletion chain, shipped inside the crate openmc-endf-8-depletion-lib-b and read into boon-lay’s DecayLibrary when one is made: for each nuclide, its half-life (none means stable), its decay energy, and each branch’s mode, daughter and ratio. About 70 unit tests compare parsed half-lives with handbook values at 1 % or better (for example H-3 12.32 y, I-131 8.02 d, Cs-137 30.17 y). Those check that the data were read right; they are not Monte Carlo checks.

Two things worth knowing. The library’s random generator is seeded with a constant, 77, so every new library replays the same sequence: runs are reproducible, but two libraries are not independent. And TRISO-ATOPS uses different half-lives: its 84-nuclide table comes from the IAEA Live Chart (chapter 5), so the two halves of boon-lay do not share one decay-data source.

One atom through a whole chain

A time step in a simulation may be long enough for an atom to decay several times. So at birth the simulator samples the atom’s whole future: a branch at every step and a lifetime for every nuclide on the way. A time step then spends those lifetimes in order until it runs out. That is allowed because decay has no memory: sampling a lifetime at birth or when the nuclide is reached gives the same distribution.

Kr-88 shows it: its daughter Rb-88 lives only about a tenth as long, so in the widget’s steps of 0.05 Kr-88 half-lives many atoms pass through Rb-88 and on to stable Sr-88 within one step. The tutorial noted that no test checked this against the coupled decay law; here every species of all five chains, stepped 100 times, stays within … standard deviations of the CRAM48 expected inventory built from the same library (predicted: within 4).

Many atoms make the decay law

Where is N(t) = N0 e−λt in this code? Nowhere. There is no function for it. It emerges when many independent atoms are counted. The surviving fraction of … atoms stays within … standard deviations of 2−t/T½ for every parent (predicted: within 4, worst about 2 to 3). For a chain the coupled decay laws of parents and daughters (the Bateman equations) emerge the same way, with no matrix: the code just counts identities across an ensemble.

That is the point of the Lagrangian approach. A burnup code solves dN/dt = AN with a large, stiff matrix. Here each atom samples waiting times, and the population is a histogram. The price is statistics: a fraction counted from N atoms has a binomial error √(p(1−p)/N), and a nuclide present at one atom in a million needs millions of histories to be seen at all. A matrix method has no such floor. That is why boon-lay keeps CRAM48 beside the Monte Carlo as its deterministic check, not as its simulator.

The crate’s own check, and what a pass means

boon-lay’s test stochastic_half_life_calculator draws 99 999 lifetimes for a 30 s half-life (seed 77) and compares the fraction alive at every second from 0 to 100 s with 2−t/30, at 0.9 % relative. Its record (boon-lay’s CLAUDE.md, re-measured 2026-10-04): worst point 8.43×10−3 relative at 60 s, 1.5 σ. At 100 s only about 10 % survive and one standard deviation is about 0.95 % relative, larger than the tolerance: on another seed some point would quite likely fail with nothing wrong in the code. That is a reason to state what a pass means (within about one standard deviation, for this seed), not to widen the tolerance silently. It is verification against an analytic law, not validation.

Where it is in the code

  • One lifetime: get_time_to_decay_stochastic, three lines: |ln ξ|/ln 2 × T½, with ξ from the workspace’s generator (Lcg64::rand_float in PETIR, which the transport track explains).
  • One branch: StochasticDecayChain::new_single_stochastic_chain_from_nuclide looks the nuclide up, draws one ξ from the library’s generator, and calls get_next_target_nuclide_with_float, the bar, until it reaches a stable nuclide.
  • The data: DecayLibrary::new, one call per element, through parse_nuclides_to_decay_data_vec_by_element to From<SerdeNuclideData>.
  • The whole future and the stepping: new_decay_chain_simulation and advance_timestep (three cases per nuclide: the step ends before the decay, exactly at it, or after it, carrying the remainder on).
  • Counting: count_nuclides_in_sims_linear; and the deterministic check, ExpectedInventory::from_library (CRAM48 on the matrix the sampler implies).

How the page’s numbers were checked

The generator wrote its predictions down before its first run (crates/dhoby-ghaut/examples/lesson_decay_walk/main.rs, D1–D6) and the results below them, unedited. The yardstick σ = √(p(1−p)/N) is used only where at least 10 atoms are expected on each side; Ba-137m, which lives a few minutes beside a 30-year parent, is present at about one atom in ten million, so its count is shown but not scored.

The CRAM48 check is the sampler’s own expectation

ExpectedInventory builds its matrix from the branch the sampler would pick, not from the tabulated ratio: the bar is walked in order, so if the ratios added to more than 1 the later branches would be cut short in both. It is a check that the sampler and the stepping do what the data say, not a second evaluation.
