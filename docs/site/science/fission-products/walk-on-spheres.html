Walking on spheres

Fission products

Lessons 9 and 10 built diffusion from single hops: billions a second, each a fraction of a nanometre. Nobody can follow every one of them for hours. A simulator needs a shortcut. The obvious one fails inside a TRISO particle; this lesson shows why, and the shortcut boon-lay uses instead.

The obvious shortcut: one Gaussian step

Add up many small random hops and the sum is a Gaussian, whatever the hops look like (the central limit theorem, lesson 9). So jump straight to the end of a time interval Δt with one Gaussian step: each of its three components has variance

σ2 = 2 D Δt

with D the diffusion coefficient. The step grows as √Δt, not Δt: the signature of diffusion. boon-lay’s first engine did exactly this. It is exact in an infinite, uniform medium.

Predict first. In the buffer, boon-lay gives caesium D = … m²/s at 1200 °C. The buffer is 100 µm thick, the IPyC around it 40 µm. With Δt = …, σ is 141 µm. What share of atoms starting in the buffer lands in the SiC or beyond in one step?

Why that fails inside a TRISO particle

At σ = 141 µm, … of the steps from the buffer land in the SiC or beyond, having jumped clean over the 40 µm IPyC without ever meeting the buffer/IPyC boundary; at 447 µm it is …, and … leave the particle altogether in one step. The step only becomes safe when it is much smaller than the thinnest layer (at 14 µm, …). But then an atom in the buffer needs hundreds of steps per simulated second, while one in the kernel, with D about 10−15 m²/s, barely moves per step. No single Δt suits both.

The lesson that generalises: the central limit theorem gives the free-space answer, and a bounded, layered domain is not free space. The failure is analysed in boon-lay’s docs/buffer_clt_failure_analysis.md; it is a diagnosis, not a V&V record.

Predict. If every step could somehow be sized so that it never reaches the nearest boundary, would the time each step takes still be fixed?

Walk-on-Spheres: hops that never cross a boundary

No. Turn the question round: fix the distance, and let the time be random. Draw the largest sphere around the atom that touches no boundary. Inside it the medium is uniform, so:

  • where the atom first reaches the sphere’s surface is uniform over it (diffusion has no preferred direction);
  • how long that takes has a known distribution.

Jump there, add that time to the atom’s clock, and repeat. Far from a boundary the hops are big; near one they shrink, and the atom is captured once it is within 10−3 of the radius of the surface. There is no time step anywhere: simulated time is the sum of the hop times.

How long does one hop take?

For a walker started at the centre of a sphere of radius R, the chance of still being inside at the dimensionless time θ = Dt/R² is

S(θ) = 2 Σk≥1 (−1)k+1 e−k²π²θ,   ⟨t⟩ = R²/(6D).

Because θ has no units, its distribution is the same for every hop of every walker. The code tabulates the inverse of 1−S once (6001 points over θ from 10−5 to 8), draws θ from it, and turns it into a time with t = θR²/D. The mean of 200 000 drawn θ is …, against 1/6 = 0.1667 (predicted: within 3 standard errors). A walker in the kernel took … hops on average before it was captured (at most …), and every recorded sphere lies inside the kernel.

Does a crowd of walkers give the right release?

The walk is built from exact pieces. Does a crowd of walkers reproduce what the diffusion equation says? On the one case with an exact answer it can be checked: the IAEA CRP-6 benchmark’s Case 1, a bare kernel with caesium spread evenly through it and a surface that removes every atom that reaches it. Crank’s series gives the fraction released by time t:

Mt/M∞ = 1 − (6/π²) Σn≥1 (1/n²) exp(−Dn²π²t/R²)

What the check does not cover

  • A disagreement it does not settle. Two of boon-lay’s tests quote Hales et al. (2021, Table 4) for Case 1a as 0.453–0.498, and 0.5337 lies outside that range; both wrap the range check so its failure is invisible. It does not touch the walker-against-Crank verification (both sides use the same D); it questions whether the crate’s Case 1a inputs are the benchmark’s. The paper is not in the open corpus, so it is not checked here: gh:#537, open.
  • One uniform sphere. No layer boundaries. The five-layer walk is exercised only qualitatively, and in practice it cannot get an atom through the coatings: lesson 34 shows boon-lay’s own walker stuck at the buffer/PyC boundary (its cost probe), so it is not repeated here. There is no full multilayer CRP-6 release record.
  • No decay during the walk. The atom’s clock (An atom’s clock) and its walk are separate here.

The same Walk-on-Spheres Monte Carlo, run on the HTR-10 kernel and compared with Booth’s series (the same series as Crank’s), is lesson 18’s check (Booth on one grain).

Where it is in the code

  • The legacy Gaussian step: scatter_within_triso_particle_gaussian_simple looks D up at the atom’s position and moves it by sample_dimensioned_gaussian_vector once per time step. It is still in the crate, beside its replacement.
  • The Case 1 check: mc_kernel_release_fraction: one D for the kernel; per history a uniform birth (sample_uniform_in_ball, r = R U1/3), a walker with a child generator, and walk_to_absorbing_sphere: hop radius = distance to the surface, stop within 10−3 R.
  • One hop: sample_first_passage_time → sample_dimensionless_exit_time (the table, built once), and sample_uniform_direction for the exit point. In a layered cell, WoSWalker::hop sizes the sphere to the nearest boundary of any layer.
  • The reference: calculate_analytical_fraction_released, Crank’s series, 200 terms.

Why the fixed step misses atoms

A walk sampled only at the end of each step overshoots a boundary, on average, by about 0.583 σ (Siegmund’s constant for a Gaussian random walk: source pending): it behaves as if the sink were that much further out. The atoms in that thin layer are missing from the early release, a deficit of roughly 3×0.583 σ/R. That estimate, written before the run, gave 0.12–0.22, 0.04–0.08 and 0.01–0.03 for the three steps; the measured deficits are in the paragraph above and the generator’s Results.
