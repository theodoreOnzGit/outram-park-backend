Breathing: how the release leaves the vessel

Fission products

The previous lesson released fission products from the fuel during an accident. But atoms that have left a kernel are still inside the reactor vessel. What carries them out? In TRISO-ATOPS the answer is one thing only: the helium the core pushes out as it heats. Stoyer et al. (Stoyer et al., 2026) call it breathing. This lesson writes the model out, then tests the one assumption that sets how much it lets out.

The physical picture

After a break in the pressure boundary, the circuit first blows down from operating pressure to the atmosphere’s. TRISO-ATOPS releases the activity that was circulating in the helium, and a chosen share of what had plated out, at once (a separate term; previous lesson). After that the vessel sits at atmospheric pressure, with nothing driving gas through the core. Then the core heats up, because decay heat is no longer removed. The gas in a fixed volume at fixed pressure must lose moles as it warms:

n = PV/RT  ⇒  fraction of the starting gas pushed out by t = 1 − T0/Tmax(t)

with Tmax the highest temperature reached so far. Heating from 900 to 1600 °C pushes out 1 − 1173/1873 = 37 % of the helium. When the core cools the gas contracts, air is drawn in, and nothing goes out: the core breathes in. There is no flow through the core in this model at any time: no air or water ingress carrying the release out (gh:#446).

How TRISO-ATOPS writes it

coolant_release integrates dn/dt = −(P/R)(dT/dt)/T² by trapezoids and divides by the starting n0. It keeps only the samples where the core is heating (dT/dt ≥ 0, a hold included). Then accident_release_curies multiplies:

released(t) = frac(t) × [kernel + graphite release by t] + circulating + xlift-off × plated out

The product in the first term is the assumption this lesson is about.

Predict first. Caesium leaves the kernels mostly during the hold at the peak, when the temperature is constant. How much of what is released during the hold does a gas that is not moving carry out of the vessel? And how much does the product formula carry out?

The assumption, and what a well-mixed gas would do

The product gives every atom released by t the whole vented share frac(t), as if it had been in the gas from the very start. Picture the gas instead as one well-mixed volume. An atom that leaves the fuel at time s joins the gas present then. It can only leave with gas pushed out after s. If s is on the way up, that share is 1 − T(s)/Tmax(t). If it is during the hold or the cool-down, the share is zero. So

carried out(t) = ∫ dR(s) [1 − T(s)/Tmax(t)]  ≤  R(t) [1 − T0/Tmax(t)]

The right-hand side is the product. The two are equal only if everything was released at the start. Otherwise the product is an upper bound: conservative in this respect, by an amount set by when the release happens.

Every assumption in the breathing model

  • The product frac × R: an upper bound on a well-mixed gas, as measured above.
  • Ideal gas at fixed pressure and volume. The pressure cancels exactly: P appears in dn/dt and in n0 alike (verified bit for bit against upstream, 2026-09-21, in coolant_release’s docs).
  • One gas temperature. The rate of change is averaged over the whole core (mean_temperature_rate), but the T in T² and in n0 is the hottest node’s. The gas is taken to be at the fuel’s temperature.
  • Nothing leaves while cooling, and nothing flows through. No ingress transport (gh:#446). sembawang adds flow-through and prescribed venting as options, labelled not upstream.
  • The first step. The trapezoid starts from dT/dt = 0, so the port vents … at 5 h where the ideal gas gives …, and … against … by the end of the hold. That is why, in the port’s own numbers, the well-mixed gas edges above the product at the first one to three samples. With the ideal-gas fraction the bound holds everywhere (check-breathing.js).
  • Upstream quirks kept: the first venting sample is set to 1, and a uniform, constant temperature vents everything (frac = 1; that branch is in sembawang’s orchestration, restored for gh:#446).

Upstream, extension, simulator: which is which

  • Upstream TRISO-ATOPS (the port): coolant_release, mean_temperature_rate, accident_release_curies, everything in the formula above.
  • boon-lay’s extension: live_pools, used here only to measure the product. TRISO-ATOPS does not have it.
  • sembawang: the venting options beyond upstream. The coupled simulator htgr_sim_v1 has its own pool transfers, which this lesson does not examine.

The predictions, and what missed

Written before the first run (lesson_breathing/main.rs, B1–B6). Held: the well-mixed run matches the written-out formula (to 4×10−9); caesium and strontium below half the product; Kr-88 below 0.6 of its no-decay value with decay, I-131 within 5 %; nothing leaves after the hold; converged in the substeps. Missed: “never above the product at any venting sample”, at the first one to three samples, for the reason above. The prediction was derived with the ideal-gas fraction, and the port’s first step is not that.

Why the well-mixed gas is not “the right answer” either

A single well-mixed volume is a second idealisation. Real gas is not mixed across a whole reactor, and the release from a hot zone may be pushed out before it mixes. It is the natural reference for this question, because it uses the same gas and the same release and drops only the product’s timing assumption. It is not a validated model of an HTGR’s vessel.
