Fission: fissile and fissionable

Neutronics

Some nuclei split with a neutron of almost any energy. Others split only when the neutron is fast. Two words keep them apart:

  • Fissile: fissions with neutrons of essentially any energy, thermal ones included. U-235, Pu-239 and U-233.
  • Fissionable but not fissile: fissions only above a threshold neutron energy. U-238, above roughly 1–1.5 MeV, rising steeply near 1.3–1.5 MeV.

The fission cross sections of U-235 and U-238

ENDF/B-VIII.0 (Brown & others, 2018), MT 18, reconstructed at 0 K by OUTRAM PARK's RECONR port (MacFarlane et al., 2017). U-238's threshold is marked where its cross section stays above 10 % of its 2 MeV value: a definition on the data, not a tabulated number.

At thermal energies U-235's fission cross section is hundreds of barns and U-238's is next to nothing. Above about a mega-electronvolt, U-238 begins to split too.

Why? Pairing, from page 9

When a neutron joins U-235 it makes U-236: 92 protons and 144 neutrons, both even. Pairing makes that nucleus extra tightly bound, so the capture releases more energy. U-238 plus a neutron makes U-239, with an odd 147 neutrons, and releases less. The evaluations' capture Q-values (MT 102) say how much: 6.54 MeV for U-235 against 4.81 MeV for U-238, about 1.7 MeV more (Krane, 1987).

To split, the compound nucleus has to get over a fission barrier. Barrier heights are not in ENDF, and they depend on the model: the actinide barriers are double-humped, and what matters is the higher hump. For U-236 it is ≈ 5.2–5.7 MeV, for U-239 ≈ 6.1–6.5 MeV (Maslov's compilation fitted to fission data, for RIPL (Capote et al., 2009), and the ETFSI calculation (Mamdouh et al., 2001), both as read from the TALYS database (Koning et al., 2026)).

  • U-235 + n: the binding energy alone lifts U-236 ≈ 0.9–1.3 MeV above its barrier, so even a neutron at rest will do.
  • U-238 + n: U-239 is left ≈ 1.3–1.6 MeV short, so the neutron has to bring roughly 1.3–1.7 MeV of kinetic energy, depending on the barrier model. That is the steep rise you can see above. The small fission below it is the nucleus tunnelling through the barrier, so the threshold is not sharp.

The same levels, every time

It is the same idea throughout. The compound nucleus has allowed energy levels (pages 8 and 9). Each reaction is a different way out of them (page 10): send the neutron back out, send it out and leave the nucleus excited, emit a gamma ray, emit a proton or an alpha particle, emit two neutrons, or split. So the same levels show up differently in each reaction's cross section.

And there are a lot of them. U-238's evaluated resolved-resonance range alone lists 926 resonances with l = 0 and 2 419 with l = 1, and that is one nuclide.

How do we represent them? Not as thousands of dense tables of points: we store a few numbers per level, and rebuild each peak from them.
