Meet the neutron

Neutronics · Lesson 1

What is neutronics?

Neutronics is the study of the behaviour of neutrons in nuclear systems.

In a nuclear reactor, neutrons move through different materials and interact with atomic nuclei. A neutron may travel through a material without interacting, scatter from a nucleus, be absorbed, or cause fission.

What's going on here?

Each coloured line is one neutron's path, from where it was born to where its history ended. The colour is the neutron's energy: it changes at a collision. The marks along and at the end of a path are what happened to it:

  • Scattering (small dots along the path). The neutron hits a nucleus and survives. Its direction and energy may change: that is what makes the path zig-zag, like a random walk.
  • Absorption (red ×). The neutron is absorbed, here without fission, and its history ends.
  • Fission (yellow dot in a ring). The neutron makes a nucleus split. New neutrons are produced and begin new histories: in the demo the next neutron starts at that spot.
  • Leakage (violet ring). The neutron leaves the uranium and never comes back.

The markers are drawn as the demo draws them; the green ring is where a neutron was born. The tracks are three-dimensional and drawn from above, so a neutron that left through the top or bottom face seems to stop inside the bar.

Why does the neutron interact with matter like that?

Begin with the easiest interaction to understand: elastic scattering, a collision in which the neutron and the nucleus only exchange motion. No energy goes into the nucleus's insides, and nothing is created or destroyed.

Elastic scattering: the classical picture

Treat the neutron and the nucleus as two balls on a table. In an elastic collision two things are conserved:

  • momentum, mn v = mn v′ + M V′, and
  • kinetic energy, ½ mn v² = ½ mn v′² + ½ M V′².

From those two laws alone, a neutron that hits a nucleus at rest head-on keeps the fraction

E′ / E = ( (A − 1) / (A + 1) )²

of its energy, where A is the nucleus's mass in neutron masses. The rest goes to the nucleus.

Predict first. To take energy away from a fast neutron, is a light nucleus (hydrogen) or a heavy one (uranium) better?

Collide a neutron with a nucleus

Masses: each nucleus's atomic weight ratio A (mass in neutron masses) as its ENDF/B-VIII.0 evaluation states it (Brown & others, 2018): H-1 0.9991673 (the H-1 tape in this repository is the ENDF/B-VIII.0 beta-6 file), C-12 11.89365, U-238 236.0058. The drawn sizes are illustrative, and the animation is not to time scale; the relative speeds before and after are.

Hydrogen can take almost all of a neutron's energy in one collision; uranium-238 takes at most about 1.7 %. That is why a reactor that wants slow neutrons surrounds its fuel with light nuclei (water, graphite): the idea behind moderation, which comes back in later lessons.

What this picture is not. The two-ball collision is intuition for the kinematics of scattering only. OUTRAM PARK, like OpenMC, does not model scattering as hard spheres: it samples the outgoing angle and energy from the evaluated angular and energy distributions in the nuclear data. And at thermal energies the target nucleus is not at rest, and may be bound in a molecule or crystal, which needs another treatment altogether.

Where the classical picture stops

The collision demo answers one question and leaves another open.

  1. If a collision happens, what happens afterwards? Classical two-body kinematics gives useful intuition: conservation of momentum and energy decide how the energy is shared.
  2. How likely is the neutron to interact with the nucleus in the first place? Here the billiard-ball picture fails. Answering it needs quantum physics.

Why isn't a nucleus just a tiny billiard ball?

A neutron and a nucleus are quantum-mechanical systems, and matter shows wave-particle duality. Two consequences matter here; neither needs Schrödinger's equation yet.

1. A moving neutron has a wavelength

De Broglie's relation gives a moving particle a wavelength set by its momentum p:

λ = h / p   ≈   h / (mn v)  (slow, non-relativistic neutron)

The slower the neutron, the longer its matter wavelength. A thermal neutron (0.0253 eV, about 2200 m/s) has λ ≈ 1.8 Å, the size of an atom; a 1 MeV fission neutron has λ ≈ 29 fm. (Computed in the demo below from the CODATA 2018 constants (Tiesinga et al., 2021).)

Careful: a longer wavelength does not mean the neutron is more smeared out in space. Wavelength and localisation are different quantities, and the demo draws them separately.

A neutron as a wave packet

A free-particle Gaussian wave packet, computed exactly: the carrier wave's wavelength is the de Broglie λ = h/(mnv); the envelope's width is how localised the neutron is, which you set separately. Animated, the packet moves at the neutron's speed and slowly spreads, while the crests inside it move at half that speed (the phase velocity of a free particle). Constants: CODATA 2018 (Tiesinga et al., 2021).

Optional: Heisenberg's uncertainty relation

Turn on Heisenberg in the wave-packet controls. The lower strip shows the spread of the neutron's speed that belongs to the packet above it. Make the packet narrower (more localised) and the spread of speeds gets broader, and the other way round: for this Gaussian packet, σx σp = ℏ/2, the smallest the uncertainty relation allows.

The relation is about the two spreads. It says nothing about the neutron's mean speed: a slow neutron is not, because it is slow, more spread out in space.

2. A nucleus has discrete energy levels

Just as electrons in an atom occupy discrete quantum states, a nucleus has discrete quantum energy levels: a ground state and excited states above it, with nothing allowed in between. So a neutron meets a quantum system with allowed states, not a featureless sphere.

A standing wave gives the intuition. A wave confined to a region can only fit a whole number of half-wavelengths, so only certain patterns, and certain energies, are allowed.

Only some states fit

An illustration: a wave in a one-dimensional box, whose allowed energies grow as n². It is the intuition for why bound quantum systems have levels, not a model of any nucleus; real nuclear levels are measured, and their spacing is irregular.

Discrete levels are what later lessons build on: nuclear excitation, resonances (a neutron whose energy matches a level of the combined nucleus is far more likely to interact), inelastic scattering and the compound nucleus.

A longer wavelength at low energy is useful intuition for why slow neutrons interact differently, but the wavelength alone does not derive the "1/v" law of absorption at low energy. That needs the reaction physics, in a later lesson.
