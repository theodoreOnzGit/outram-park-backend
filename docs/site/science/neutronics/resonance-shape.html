What shapes a resonance peak?

Neutronics

Page 11 ended with a problem: U-238 alone has thousands of resonances. Storing every peak as a dense table of points would be enormous. Instead, an evaluation stores a few numbers per level and a formula that rebuilds the peak from them.

For one level those numbers are:

  • Er, the energy where the peak sits;
  • J, the level's spin, with the neutron's orbital angular momentum l (l = 0 is an s-wave, l = 1 a p-wave);
  • the partial widths: Γn for leaving by re-emitting the neutron, Γγ for leaving by gamma rays, Γf for splitting. They are the exits of page 10, one width each.

The simplest formula treats each level as if it were alone. It is the single-level Breit–Wigner (SLBW) formula (Breit & Wigner, 1936), ENDF's LRF = 1 (Trkov et al., 2018).

One level, rebuilt from its numbers

The level is U-238's lowest s-wave resonance in ENDF/B-VIII.0 (Brown & others, 2018): Er = 6.674 eV, J = ½, Γn = 1.49 meV, Γγ = 22.7 meV. The curve is NJOY2016's csslbw (MacFarlane et al., 2017) through OUTRAM PARK's RECONR port, at 0 K (no Doppler broadening). The evaluation gives these numbers for the Reich–Moore formula (page 14), so this SLBW drawing is an approximation of it.

Things to try:

  • Make Γγ bigger. The peak gets wider and lower. The full width at half maximum is Γ = Γn + Γγ (+ Γf).
  • Switch to elastic. Just below the peak the cross section dips. That dip is the resonance interfering with the hard-sphere (potential) scattering of page 5: two waves, adding with a phase that flips as the energy passes Er.

A width is a lifetime

Why should a level have a width at all? Because it does not last. A compound nucleus that lives for a time τ has an energy that is uncertain by about Γ = ℏ/τ. A short-lived level is a wide peak; a long-lived level is a narrow one. For U-238's 6.67 eV level, Γ ≈ 24 meV gives τ ≈ 27 femtoseconds: the lifetime of the compound nucleus of page 8, from its formation to one of the exits of page 10.

Each partial width is a share of that decay rate. Γγ / Γ is the chance that the level ends in capture; Γn / Γ the chance that it re-emits the neutron. For this level that is 94 % capture, which is why U-238 is a strong absorber at 6.67 eV.

Deeper: the SLBW formulas

For one level r of spin J in the l-th partial wave, at neutron energy E, with wave number k and statistical factor gJ = (2J + 1) / (2(2I + 1)) for a target of spin I (ENDF-102 App. D.1.1):

σγ(E) = (π/k²) gJ Γn(E) Γγ / [ (E − E′r)² + Γ(E)²/4 ]

and the same with Γf for fission. Elastic adds the potential scattering and an interference term:

σn(E) = (4π/k²)(2l + 1) sin²φl + (π/k²) gJ Γn [ Γn cos 2φl − 2(Γγ + Γf) sin²φl + 2(E − E′r) sin 2φl ] / [ (E − E′r)² + Γ²/4 ]

φl is the hard-sphere phase shift. The neutron width changes with energy through the penetrability Pl(E): Γn(E) = Γn(Er) Pl(E) / Pl(Er). E′r is Er moved slightly by the shift factor (zero for s-waves). The term with sin 2φl changes sign at the resonance: that is the elastic dip.

Why is this so? And what about the others?

SLBW treats each level as if the others did not exist, and adds the peaks. That is fine when the levels are far apart compared with their widths. But U-238 has thousands of them, some close together.
