What does a neutron wave do at a nucleus?

Neutronics

On the last page the neutron became a wave. So meeting a nucleus is a wave meeting an obstacle, and a wave does not bounce off an obstacle the way a ball does: it spreads out from it, in all directions at once, and the parts of the spreading wave interfere.

incoming neutron wave → nuclear scattering potential → partial waves → interference → a probability for each outgoing direction

Predict first. A slow neutron has a wavelength far bigger than the nucleus; a fast one, comparable or smaller. Which of the two scatters equally in every direction, and which prefers to keep going forward?

A neutron wave meets a nucleus

Exact quantum scattering, by partial waves, from the simplest nuclear potential there is: an impenetrable sphere. Its radius is each nucleus's scattering radius AP from its ENDF/B-VIII.0 evaluation (Brown & others, 2018): C-12 6.147 fm, O-16 5.495 fm, U-238 9.48 fm. The phase shifts are the hard-sphere ones NJOY uses for potential scattering (facphi, reconr.f90) (MacFarlane et al., 2017), and the wave number its cwaven. Drawn in the centre-of-mass frame; teal and brown are the crests and troughs of the wave. At low energy this model's cross section is 4πAP², which equals the evaluations' elastic cross section (C-12 4.748 b, O-16 3.794 b), but by construction: evaluators choose AP so that it does. It is not a prediction.

Slide the energy and watch the polar plot. At low energy the wavelength dwarfs the nucleus, only one partial wave (l = 0) scatters, and every direction is equally likely. As the energy rises, more partial waves join in, they interfere, and the distribution leans forward, with lobes where they add and dips where they cancel.

The neutron does not simply bounce from the nucleus like a classical billiard ball. Quantum-mechanical scattering waves interfere, making some outgoing directions more probable than others.

A careful distinction. A simple transport model might assume isotropic scattering, where every outgoing direction is equally likely: the dashed circle in the plot. That is a simplifying model. Real neutron–nucleus scattering is governed by quantum mechanics and may be anisotropic, as the plot shows.

What this picture is not. A real nucleus is not impenetrable. The neutron can enter it, be absorbed, or form a compound nucleus that lives for a while and re-emits it, and near a resonance that can change the angular distribution a great deal. The impenetrable sphere is the smooth background the resonance formulas start from, not the whole answer.

Press Sample one scattering angle. The program draws one outgoing direction at random, with the probabilities of the polar plot, and the neutron leaves along it. That is what a Monte Carlo code does at every collision: it does not follow the wave, it samples one outcome from the distribution.

Does OutramMC solve Schrödinger's equation at every collision?

No. That would be far too expensive: a reactor calculation follows millions of neutrons through millions of collisions, and the impenetrable sphere above is the easiest scattering problem there is. Instead, the physics is done once, ahead of time:

quantum nuclear physics → measurement, calculation and evaluation → nuclear data → OutramMC → one sampled outcome per collision

We calculate, measure and evaluate the relevant nuclear behaviour beforehand and store the results as nuclear data. The transport code only reads it and samples from it.
