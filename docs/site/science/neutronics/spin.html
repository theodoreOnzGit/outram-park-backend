Maths 5i: why does each hydrogen scatter differently?

Neutronics

Rung 5h: hydrogen scatters almost completely incoherently, carbon and oxygen coherently. Why?

Neutrons and nuclei spin. A neutron behaves like a tiny spinning top: it has an intrinsic spin of ½. Many nuclei spin too; the proton, hydrogen's nucleus, also has spin ½.

The strength of the collision depends on how the spins line up. When a neutron meets a proton their spins can point the same way or opposite ways, and the nuclear force acts very differently in the two cases. The total spin 1 arrangement is called the triplet, the total spin 0 one the singlet. Their scattering lengths, the "strength" of the scattered wave, are 10.817 fm and −47.420 fm: different in size and even in sign (NIST Center for Neutron Research, 2026), (Sears, 1992). It is page 12's total angular momentum J again: the neutron's spin and the nucleus's combine into J, and different J behave differently.

Counting the arrangements. Total spin 1 has 3 states and total spin 0 has 1, so an unpolarised neutron meets a triplet 3 times in 4 and a singlet 1 time in 4: page 12's statistical factor gJ = (2J + 1)/(2(2I + 1)) with I = ½. Weighted that way, the average length is ¾ × 10.817 + ¼ × (−47.420) = −3.742 fm, the tabulated coherent length of hydrogen-1 (−3.7395 fm).

Spins and the waves they scatter

Rung 5h's patch of atoms, with each atom's scattering length from the NIST table. "Line all spins up" is an illustration: it shows that the randomness of the spins is what kills the beams.

In a sample the nuclear spins point every which way. Each hydrogen nucleus presents the neutron with a triplet-like or a singlet-like strength, at random, so the scattered waves don't keep in step: rung 5h's incoherent case. That is why hydrogen is incoherent. Carbon-12 and oxygen-16 have spin zero (NIST Center for Neutron Research, 2026): every nucleus looks the same to the neutron, the waves keep in step, and the scattering is coherent.

A mixture of isotopes, each with its own strength, makes atoms look different too: a second source of incoherence.

The split, in plain words. The average strength over all the atoms gives the part that can interfere: coherent. The spread about that average gives the part that can't: incoherent. For hydrogen the average is small and the spread huge: its coherent cross section is 1.7583 b, its incoherent one 80.27 b. Carbon-12's incoherent cross section is 0.

Deeper: the two cross sections from the two lengths

With the 3:1 weights, the coherent cross section is 4π⟨b⟩² and the incoherent one 4π(⟨b²⟩ − ⟨b⟩²): the average and the spread. From the triplet and singlet lengths above these give 1.76 b and 79.9 b, close to the tabulated values.

A confirmation: liquid hydrogen

In a hydrogen molecule the two protons' spins are correlated (ortho- and para-hydrogen), which "violate[s] the assumption of randomness, so liquid hydrogen does not fit into the model described here" (NJOY2016 manual, §24): LEAPR treats it separately. The randomness of the spins really is what makes hydrogen incoherent.

Common misconception: "Incoherent scattering happens because the crystal is disordered."

Not for hydrogen. Even in a perfect crystal, each proton's spin is random relative to the neutron's, so each one scatters a little differently.

"Spin" here is the spin of the nucleus. The neutron also feels the magnetism of unpaired electrons, a different (magnetic) interaction that does not matter for these moderators.
