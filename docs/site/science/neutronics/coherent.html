Maths 5h: do the waves add up?

Neutronics

A neutron is a wave (page 4). When it passes through a crystal, every atom sends out its own little scattered wave. Do those waves add up, or not?

  • The waves keep in step. If all the atoms are identical and sit in a perfect repeating pattern, their scattered waves keep in step. In certain directions the crests line up and add up into a strong beam; in others they cancel. It is the interference of pages 5 and 13, and diffraction off the crystal's planes: the Bragg scattering of page 21, with its edges. When the waves from different atoms interfere like this, the scattering is called coherent.
  • The waves don't keep in step. If each nucleus scatters a little differently, a different strength or sign from one atom to the next, the waves have no fixed relationship and don't line up. They add as separate contributions, spread in all directions, with no Bragg peaks. That is incoherent scattering: no interference between atoms.

A patch of atoms, scattering

Real materials. NJOY's manual: "the scattering from hydrogen is almost completely incoherent, and the scattering from carbon and oxygen is almost completely coherent" (MacFarlane et al., 2017). The reason it gives is "a random distribution of spins or isotope types" from atom to atom. So:

  • graphite, carbon in a crystal, shows Bragg edges (page 21);
  • water's hydrogen scatters incoherently, so water shows no Bragg edges;
  • ZrH, hydrogen in a solid, has incoherent elastic scattering (page 21's second kind).

Why this matters for the earlier pages. For an incoherent scatterer there is no interference to keep track of, so all that matters is how much energy each collision trades: that is why the density of states and rungs 3–5 are enough. For coherent scattering in a crystal, the Bragg (zero-phonon) part needs the crystal's structure itself: page 21's coherent elastic.

Deeper: coherent inelastic

Coherent inelastic scattering has both interference terms and direct terms; for gases, liquids and randomly oriented crystallites the direct part "has approximately the same form as the incoherent term. The interference is usually neglected" (NJOY2016 manual, §24).

Common misconception: "Coherent means organised, incoherent means a messy crystal."

No: it is about whether the scattered waves from different atoms interfere. A perfect crystal of a hydrogen-containing material can still scatter mostly incoherently, because each hydrogen nucleus scatters a little differently.
