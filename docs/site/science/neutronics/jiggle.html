Maths 1½: let the nucleus jiggle

Neutronics

Take rung 1's collision and switch on thermal motion of the target: the nucleus drifts a little toward or away from the neutron, at random. Now the same kick gives a slightly different energy change every time.

Collisions with jiggling nuclei

The dots scatter around the recoil line and form a band, and the band widens as the material heats up. This is Doppler broadening again (page 22): target motion smears what would otherwise be sharp. On page 22 it smeared a resonance in energy; here it smears the recoil line.

Why an exponential appears

  • At temperature T, each velocity component of the nuclei follows a bell curve (a normal, or Gaussian, distribution): the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, with probability proportional to exp(−Mv²/2kT) (page 3).
  • For a given kick, the extra energy change depends in a straight line on the nucleus's velocity along the kick.
  • A straight-line function of a bell-curve quantity is a bell curve too. So the band is a bell curve, and a bell curve is an exp(−(something)²).

Every exponential in the free-gas maths that follows is that bell curve.

Conventions on these pages. β = (E′ − E)/kT is positive when the neutron gains energy; α measures the size of the kick. S is LEAPR's asymmetric scattering law (the NJOY2016 manual's script S); ENDF stores the symmetric S = eβ/2 times it (page 21).

"Jiggle" here means the atoms drifting in random directions, as a gas does. Vibrating about a fixed place, on a spring, is a different motion: rung 3.
