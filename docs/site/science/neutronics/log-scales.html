Why are cross sections drawn on log scales?

Neutronics

Every cross-section plot that spans the full energy range in these lessons, on pages 9 and 11, used a logarithmic energy axis. Resonances sit at particular energies, so why not a plain one?

A neutron loses a fraction, not an amount

Go back to page 2's collision. A neutron scattering elastically off a nucleus of mass ratio A keeps between αE and all of its energy E, where α = ((A − 1)/(A + 1))². If the scattering is equally likely in every direction in the centre-of-mass frame, every energy in that range is equally likely.

So what a collision takes is a fraction of the energy the neutron had, not a fixed amount. A 2 MeV neutron and a 2 eV neutron hitting carbon lose, on average, the same fraction: about 14 %.

This holds for elastic scattering off a free nucleus at rest, isotropic in the centre-of-mass frame. Anisotropic scattering (page 7) shifts the average. Inelastic scattering (page 10) also takes a fixed amount, the level energy. Near thermal energies the nucleus's own motion matters (page 3).

So the natural axis is log E

On a log axis, equal fractions are equal distances. So every collision moves the neutron about the same distance to the left, whatever its energy. That distance, averaged over collisions, is the mean step in ln E:

ξ = 1 + α ln α / (1 − α)

It depends only on the target, not on the energy. (Reactor physicists measure this distance as lethargy, u = ln(E0/E): it grows by ξ per collision, on average.) With each evaluation's own mass ratio:

The cross section needs a log axis too

Turn the σ axis to linear and the resonance peaks squash everything else to zero. Cross sections span many decades. In U-238's resolved range, its capture cross section runs from about 0.001 b between resonances to 27 000 b at the top of a peak. B-10 (n,α) runs from 190 000 b at 10−5 eV to 0.03 b at 20 MeV.

And one rule becomes a straight line. At low energy, an absorption cross section with no threshold, such as capture or B-10's (n,α), falls roughly as 1/v, the inverse of the neutron's speed, so as E−1/2. On log–log axes that is a straight line of slope −1/2. Fitting ln σ against ln E on B-10's evaluated (n,α) between 1 meV and 1 eV gives a slope of −0.5001. Take it as the observed low-energy law: page 4 already said the wavelength alone does not derive it. Elastic scattering does not follow it: carbon's is flat at 4.748 b over the same range.

On a log-energy axis, a slowing-down neutron moves in evenly spaced steps, the same average step ξ per collision at any energy. That is why we draw neutron energies, and cross sections, on log scales.

What happens to the resonances on a log axis?

Turn on U-238's resonance ticks. On a log axis they bunch up towards high energy. On a linear axis they look evenly spaced, and they are, on average:

The mean s-wave spacing is 23.5 eV below 1 keV and 23.1 eV at 10–20 keV: constant in energy. Why? A resonance is a level of the compound nucleus at the excitation Sn + E (page 8), with Sn ≈ 4.8 MeV for U-239 (page 11). Across the whole resolved range that excitation changes by only 20 keV, so the density of levels (page 9) hardly changes. On a log axis a constant spacing D becomes a gap of about D/E in ln E, which shrinks as 1/E: that is the bunching. Per decade of energy, the s-wave count is 1, 4, 38 and 421, and 431 more between 10 and 20 keV.

  • Low energy: isolated traps. From 6.67 to 20.9 eV, U-238's first two s-wave resonances, is a gap of 1.14 in ln E: about 135 U-238 collisions, or 7 carbon ones. A neutron slowing down meets these resonances one at a time.
  • High energy: many resonances per step. At 10–20 keV one carbon collision, on average, jumps across about 99 s-wave resonances; a U-238 collision about 5.

Whether a slowing-down neutron gets past the resonances without being captured has a name, resonance escape. It is a later lesson.

Two cautions. "Evenly spaced" is true on average and within each spin group: single spacings scatter randomly, and the p-wave levels have their own, closer spacing (7.8 eV at 10–20 keV) but are mostly weak at low energy. And the bunching is a property of the plot, not of the physics: at 20 keV, U-238's resonances are still far narrower than their spacing, and even the Doppler width at 300 K, about √(4EkT/A) ≈ 3.0 eV, is well below 23 eV.

Why is this so? And higher up?

U-238's resolved range stops at 20 keV. Above it, up to 149 keV, the evaluation lists no single resonances, only their averages (Trkov et al., 2018). The resonances there still do not overlap; it is the measurements that can no longer separate them: "experimental resolution is inadequate to determine the parameters of individual resonances" (ENDF-102, §2.1).
