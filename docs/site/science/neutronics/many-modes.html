Maths 4: many modes, many phonons

Neutronics

On rung 3 every term of the phonon expansion was a sharp spike, because one swing has one step size. A real lattice has many modes, with the density of states ρ (page 24). So each term is now a spread of energies: the 1-phonon term is ρ-shaped, the 2-phonon term is ρ convolved with itself (rung 3½), and so on. It is the same phonon expansion with smeared terms, and it is what NJOY's LEAPR computes.

Build it by sampling

The phonon energies are drawn from Si in α-quartz's spectrum (its LEAPR deck); the brown curves are OUTRAM PARK's port of LEAPR (MacFarlane et al., 2017). We sample it to see it; LEAPR computes it exactly, by integration, not by sampling.

Dice first: the totals of more and more dice pile into a smoother, more bell-shaped curve. Then the same with phonons: one phonon's energy drawn from ρ (the small right-hand side is up-scatter, absorbing a phonon already there), then n phonons added, then a whole collision: a Poisson number of phonons, their energies added.

Common misconception: "It's Gaussian, not Poisson."

Underneath, the number of phonons is always Poisson. But when many phonons are added together, the central limit theorem applies and the result becomes Gaussian.

There are two different random things in one collision: how many phonons (Poisson, rung 3), and their total energy (the sum of that many draws from ρ, a convolution, rung 3½). A gentle kick makes a phonon a rare event: mostly 0, sometimes 1, lopsided. A hard kick makes many, and two effects push the result to a bell: a Poisson count with a large mean is itself bell-shaped, and adding many random energies is too. Rare events look Poisson; many events add up to a bell curve.

Conventions on these pages. β = (E′ − E)/kT is positive when the neutron gains energy; α measures the size of the kick. S is LEAPR's asymmetric scattering law (the NJOY2016 manual's script S); ENDF stores the symmetric S = eβ/2 times it (page 21).

On average every collision still loses the free recoil: the widget's running mean heads for −α, the sum rule of rung 2.

Why is this so? And huge kicks?

For a hard kick the neutron makes hundreds of phonons, and adding hundreds of cases gives a bell curve anyway. Why not use the bell curve directly?
