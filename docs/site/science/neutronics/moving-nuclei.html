The nucleus is moving too

Neutronics

In the collision demo the nucleus sat still until the neutron arrived. Real nuclei never do. The atoms of any material at a temperature above absolute zero are in constant thermal motion, and a nucleus moves with its atom.

So what decides how a collision goes is not the neutron's velocity alone but its velocity relative to the nucleus:

vrel = v − V

where v is the neutron's velocity and V the nucleus's. The chain the next demo shows:

temperature → thermal motion of the nuclei → a spread of relative velocities → a spread of encounter energies

Predict first. A slow (thermal, 0.0253 eV) neutron meets carbon at room temperature. Does every encounter happen at the neutron's own energy? Would it matter more for a 1 keV neutron, or less?

Nuclei in thermal motion

The nuclei are a free gas: each component of a nucleus's velocity is drawn from a Gaussian of spread √(kT/M), the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, with M from each nucleus's ENDF/B-VIII.0 atomic weight ratio (Brown & others, 2018) and k from CODATA 2018 (Tiesinga et al., 2021). The encounter energy is ½mn|v − V|², the energy the neutron would have if the nucleus stood still. The histogram counts nuclei; how often each pair actually meets also grows with the relative speed, which it leaves out.

Three things to see:

  • A hotter material spreads the encounters wider. At 0 K every encounter is at the neutron's own energy; heat the material and the encounter energies smear out around it.
  • Light nuclei move faster. At the same temperature, hydrogen's spread is far wider than uranium's, because its mass is smaller.
  • It matters most for slow neutrons. A thermal neutron moves at about the same speed as the nuclei, so the encounter energy can be almost anything. A 1 keV neutron is so much faster that the nuclei hardly change it.

This smearing is the root of Doppler broadening: when the chance of an interaction changes sharply with energy, the thermal motion of the nuclei blurs it. It comes back when the lessons reach cross sections and NJOY's BROADR.

Next question

The relative velocity tells us about the encounter. It does not tell us whether the neutron and the nucleus interact at all. The billiard-ball picture has no answer to that.

  1. If a collision happens, what happens afterwards? Classical kinematics gives useful intuition: conservation of momentum and energy decide how the energy is shared.
  2. How likely is the neutron to interact with the nucleus in the first place? Here the billiard-ball picture fails. Why isn't a nucleus just a tiny billiard ball?
