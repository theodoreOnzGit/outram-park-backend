Do neighbouring peaks interfere?

Neutronics

Yes. And in the exact theory, everything interferes with everything.

Page 5 showed a neutron as a wave. What quantum mechanics adds up is not probabilities but amplitudes, and the probability is the amplitude squared. Two levels give two amplitudes, A1 and A2, and

|A1 + A2|² = |A1|² + |A2|² + 2 Re(A1* A2)

The first two terms are the two peaks on their own: what page 12's SLBW adds up. The third is the interference. It can be positive or negative, so between two levels the cross section can be higher or lower than the two peaks summed.

But only levels of the same spin group interfere. Total angular momentum J (with its parity) is conserved, so a level of one J and a level of another J lead to different outgoing waves, which cannot add (Trkov et al., 2018). For them the third term is zero.

Two levels, interfering or not

Two made-up s-wave levels at 10 eV and above it, each Γn = 1 eV and Γγ = 30 meV, on a target of mass 100 and spin ½. The interfering curve is the Reich–Moore formula (page 14), NJOY2016's csrmat (MacFarlane et al., 2017) through OUTRAM PARK's RECONR port: no code here evaluates the full R-matrix with explicit gamma channels. For these levels the two differ only in how gamma rays are treated, which is next page's question. 0 K.

Things to try:

  • Same J, close together. Between and around the levels the two curves part: that gap is the interference term.
  • Different J. The curves lie on top of each other. No interference, whatever the spacing.
  • Same J, far apart. The curves come together again near each peak: interference matters most where the levels overlap.

The exact theory: R-matrix

R-matrix theory writes this down exactly (Wigner & Eisenbud, 1947), (Lane & Thomas, 1958). Every way in or out of the compound nucleus is a channel: the neutron, each fission path, each gamma-ray path. Then, for each spin group:

  1. build a matrix with one row and one column per channel;
  2. every element is a sum over all the levels of that spin group;
  3. invert the matrix, at every energy, to get the cross sections.

ENDF-6 can even store it this way: its R-Matrix Limited format with KRM = 4 is "R-Matrix without approximations", with each gamma channel on an equal footing with the others (Trkov et al., 2018).

OOOF: brute force

Let's count. U-238's evaluated resolved range lists 926 levels with l = 0 and 2 419 with l = 1, and every one of them goes into the matrix elements of its spin group.

The channel count is the real problem. A heavy compound nucleus can shed its energy through a great many different gamma cascades. Suppose, as an illustration only, that there were 1 000 gamma channels. The matrix is then 1 001 × 1 001. Inverting an n × n matrix takes about n³ operations, so that is about 109 operations, for one spin group, at one energy. Reconstructing a cross section takes thousands to millions of energies.

So brute force is the exact reference, and not something anyone runs for a heavy nucleus. None of the four R-Matrix Limited evaluations here (Fe-54, Fe-57, Cu-63, Cu-65) does it: all four use the simplification on the next page (KRM = 3; it is the only form OUTRAM PARK's port reads, and it reconstructs all four).

Deeper: the collision matrix

Each partial cross section from the neutron channel n to a channel b comes from the collision matrix U (ENDF-102 App. D.1.3):

σnb = (π/k²) gJ |δnb − Unb|²

U is built from the level matrix, a matrix over channels whose elements sum over the levels r: Σr Γnr½ Γbr½ / (Er − E − …), which is then inverted. The square root of a width is an amplitude, and the sum over r is where the levels interfere. Squaring |δ − U| at the end is the |A1 + A2|² above.

Why is this so? Can we simplify?

The exact theory is clear, and unaffordable. Most of the cost is the gamma channels, and the gamma rays are not what a reactor physicist needs one by one: what matters is that the neutron was captured.
