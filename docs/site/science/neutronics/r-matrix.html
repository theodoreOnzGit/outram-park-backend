Do neighbouring peaks interfere?

Neutronics

Yes, when they can. And in the exact theory, everything interferes with everything. Corrected 2026-10-19: two levels interfere only when they share the same total angular momentum and parity (the same spin group) and lead through the same entrance and exit channels. Levels that differ in either cannot interfere, whatever theory is used.

Add amplitudes, not cross sections

Page 5 showed a neutron as a wave. Quantum mechanics adds up amplitudes, not probabilities. If two levels each give an amplitude for the same process, the amplitude for that process is their sum:

A = A1 + A2

This is linear: the equations of quantum mechanics stay linear in the amplitudes. What we measure, though, is a cross section, and a cross section is proportional to the amplitude squared:

|A|² = |A1 + A2|² = |A1|² + |A2|² + 2 Re(A1 A2*)

The first two terms are each level's own peak: what you get by adding the two cross sections, which is what page 12's SLBW does. The third, 2 Re(A1 A2*), is the interference term. Adding cross sections means keeping |A1|² + |A2|² and silently dropping it.

Why peaks and dips. Each amplitude is a complex number whose phase turns by about half a turn as the energy passes through its level. Where the two amplitudes point the same way, the interference term is positive: constructive, and the cross section rises above the sum of the peaks. Where they point opposite ways it is negative: destructive, and the cross section falls below the sum, which can carve a dip between two levels. Nothing about the physics became nonlinear; the dip comes from squaring a sum.

When the third term is zero. Total angular momentum J and parity are conserved, so a level of one Jπ and a level of another lead to different outgoing waves, which cannot add (Trkov et al., 2018). Their amplitudes never meet in the same sum, and their cross sections do simply add.

Two levels: coherent, incoherent, and the difference

Three curves. Coherent (solid): the amplitudes added, then squared. Incoherent (dashed): each level's peak on its own, summed. Interference (dotted): their difference, Δσint(E) = σcoherent(E) − σincoherent(E); above zero it is constructive, below zero destructive. Two made-up s-wave levels at 10 eV and above it, each Γn = 1 eV and Γγ = 30 meV, on a target of mass 100 and spin ½. The coherent curve is the Reich–Moore formula (page 14), NJOY2016's csrmat (MacFarlane et al., 2017), through OUTRAM PARK's RECONR port; the incoherent curve is the SLBW sum (csslbw). It is not a full R-matrix calculation: no code here evaluates the R-matrix with explicit gamma channels. For a lone level of these the two formulas agree (checked to 1 part in 109), so Δσint here is the level–level interference alone. 0 K.

Things to try:

  • Same J, close together. The dotted curve dips well below zero between the levels (destructive: the dip in the solid curve) and sits slightly above zero outside them (constructive).
  • Different J. The dotted curve lies flat on zero and the other two coincide. No interference, whatever the spacing.
  • Same J, far apart. The dotted curve shrinks towards zero near each peak: interference matters most where the levels overlap.

How the formalisms treat interference

Every formalism on the next pages answers one question differently: which amplitudes does it add before squaring, and in which reactions? In ENDF's terms (Trkov et al., 2018), (MacFarlane et al., 2017):

SLBW (LRF 1, page 12)none: each level on its own, summednoneeach level independent
MLBW (LRF 2, page 15)yes: the neutron amplitudes of a spin group are added before squaringnone: single-level peaks, summedno channel matrix; a limit valid when Γ/D is small
Reich–Moore (LRF 3, page 14)yesfission: yes, through the channel matrix; capture: obtained as the remainder, with the many gamma channels eliminated into one width per levelneutron and up to two fission channels in a small matrix; gamma channels eliminated
R-Matrix Limited (LRF 7)yesas Reich–Moore when KRM = 3 (the eliminated gamma width); every channel explicit when KRM = 4any number and type of channels, charged particles included

MLBW is not Reich–Moore with more lumping. It is a different approximation: it keeps the multi-level sum only in the elastic amplitude and treats capture and fission one level at a time, with no channel matrix at all. Reich–Moore instead keeps a channel matrix and drops only the coupling through the gamma channels. Page 15 shows the two side by side.

Why matrices?

Interference alone does not need a matrix. Two levels and one channel give two complex numbers to add; MLBW's elastic amplitude is exactly such a sum, one complex term per level.

A matrix appears when many levels couple to many channels at once. Each level λ connects to each channel c with its own amplitude, its reduced width amplitude γλc (the square root of a partial width, up to a penetrability factor, with a sign). Going in through one channel and out through another can pass through any level, and through the other channels on the way, so the amplitudes for every pair of channels depend on all the levels and all the couplings together. Writing that out by hand is hopeless; arranging it as matrices makes it systematic (Wigner & Eisenbud, 1947), (Lane & Thomas, 1958). Three matrices are worth telling apart:

  • The R-matrix, one row and column per channel, each element a sum over the levels of the spin group: Rcc′ = Σλ γλc γλc′ / (Eλ − E). It holds the resonance parameters.
  • The level matrix, one row and column per level, each element a sum over the channels. It is the same physics written the other way round, and the natural form when there are fewer levels than channels.
  • The collision matrix U (also called the scattering matrix), one row and column per channel: Ucc′ is the amplitude for going in through c and out through c′. The cross sections come from it. It is obtained from the R-matrix (or the level matrix) by a matrix inversion that also brings in each channel's penetrability and phase shift.

R-matrix theory (Wigner & Eisenbud, 1947), (Lane & Thomas, 1958) is the framework that ties these together. Every way in or out of the compound nucleus is a channel: the neutron, each fission path, each gamma-ray path. ENDF-6 can store it with every gamma channel on an equal footing: its R-Matrix Limited format with KRM = 4 is "R-Matrix without approximations" (Trkov et al., 2018).

R-matrix theory writes this down exactly. Corrected 2026-10-19: the framework is formally exact for the channels it includes, but a practical calculation is not: it depends on which channels are kept, on boundary conditions, on how distant levels outside the resolved range are represented, on fitted parameters and on numerics.

OOOF: brute force

Let's count. U-238's evaluated resolved range lists 926 levels with l = 0 and 2 419 with l = 1, and every one of them enters the matrix elements of its spin group.

The channel count is the real problem. A heavy compound nucleus can shed its energy through a great many different gamma cascades. Suppose, as an illustration only, that there were 1 000 gamma channels. The channel matrix is then 1 001 × 1 001, and inverting a dense n × n matrix by brute force takes about n³ operations: about 109 operations, for one spin group, at one energy, at each of the thousands to millions of energies of a reconstruction. Corrected 2026-10-19: that is a brute-force illustration, not a law: real codes exploit the structure, eliminate channels (page 14) or work with the smaller of the two matrix forms.

So the all-channel form is the reference, and not something anyone runs for a heavy nucleus. None of the four R-Matrix Limited evaluations here (Fe-54, Fe-57, Cu-63, Cu-65) does it: all four use the simplification on the next page (KRM = 3; it is the only form OUTRAM PARK's port reads, and it reconstructs all four).

Deeper: from the collision matrix to cross sections

Each partial cross section from the neutron channel n to a channel b comes from the collision matrix U (ENDF-102 App. D.1.3):

σnb = (π/k²) gJ |δnb − Unb|²

U is built from the level matrix, a matrix over channels whose elements sum over the levels r, which is then inverted. Corrected 2026-10-19: the matrix over channels whose elements sum over the levels r is the R-matrix (the level matrix runs over levels instead). In the Reich–Moore form NJOY evaluates (page 14), its elements are Σr Γnr½ Γbr½ / (Er − E − iΓγr/2), and U follows from inverting a small channel matrix built from it. The square root of a width is an amplitude, and the sum over r is where the levels interfere. Squaring |δ − U| at the end is the |A1 + A2|² above.

Where this meets RECONR

RECONR, the first NJOY module, turns resonance parameters into a pointwise cross section (MacFarlane et al., 2017). For each energy range it reads the formalism flag (LRF) and calls the matching routine: csslbw for SLBW, csmlbw for MLBW, csrmat for Reich–Moore, and the R-Matrix Limited routine for LRF 7. So which interference a reconstructed cross section contains is decided by the evaluator's choice of formalism, row by row in the table above, and RECONR reproduces that choice. The curves in the widget are those routines, through OUTRAM PARK's port; reviewing them later means checking each against the formalism its row describes.

Why is this so? Can we simplify?

The full framework is clear, and unaffordable by brute force. Most of the cost is the gamma channels, and the gamma rays are not what a reactor physicist needs one by one: what matters is that the neutron was captured.
