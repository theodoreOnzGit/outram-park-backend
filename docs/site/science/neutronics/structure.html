How does structure shape the phonons?

Neutronics

Page 24 ended on the density of states, the material's vibration fingerprint. What decides its shape? The structure: which atoms, how they are bonded, how they are arranged. Simplest first.

From a molecule to a layered crystal

Toys on page 23's lattice, except water's spectrum, read from the LEAPR input deck behind its ENDF/B-VIII.0 evaluation (Brown & others, 2018).

1. A molecule: water

Start with the smallest structure, one molecule. Its atoms are held by bonds, so it can vibrate only in its own fixed patterns. A molecule of N atoms that is not a straight line has 3N − 6 of them, so water, H2O, has 3: two stretches and a bend. Each one is quantised, a ladder of equal steps like page 23's mode (for one molecule we say a vibrational quantum; "phonon" is kept for a whole lattice). So water's internal vibrations show up as a few sharp lines in its fingerprint.

In the liquid, each molecule also rotates (hindered by its neighbours) and drifts. These add a broad, continuous part. The ENDF/B-VIII.0 model of hydrogen in water has exactly that shape: a continuous spectrum from molecular dynamics, a drift (diffusion) term, and two discrete oscillators at 0.205 and 0.415 eV. The two stretches sit close together, and the model lumps the molecule's vibrations into those two lines.

The structure, here the molecule's shape and bonds, decides which vibrations exist. And a liquid's molecules point every which way, so on average it has no preferred direction: the simpler case first.

2. A crystal

Now many atoms in a repeating lattice. The vibrations belong to the whole crystal: collective modes, the phonons of pages 23–24, spread into bands. Change the structure and the bands change. In the toy, giving every other atom three times the mass splits one band into two, the acoustic and the optical, with a gap between them where the crystal has no vibration at all. Same springs, a completely different ρ.

3. Layers: vibrations follow the structure

If the bonds differ by direction, the vibrations do too. Graphite is the clear example. It is layered: atoms are stiffly bonded within each layer and only weakly between layers. So vibrations along the layers and across them meet very different springs, and have very different frequencies and speeds. Graphite's phonons are anisotropic: they follow the crystal's structure. (It is the striking case, not the only one: even simple crystals vibrate a little differently in different directions.)

What the data actually do

The physics is anisotropic. The scattering laws used in reactor calculations mostly average that away:

  • Reactor graphite is polycrystalline: many small crystals at random angles, so the direction-dependence mostly averages out in the bulk.
  • ENDF/B-VIII.0's crystalline graphite describes "graphite (perfect single crystal)", but its MF=1 text says "the coherent elastic data were prepared using the cubic approximation in LEAPR/NJOY": a direction-averaged treatment.

So the evaluated laws are, for the most part, direction-averaged approximations. That is a simplification, and a page like this one is where it shows.
