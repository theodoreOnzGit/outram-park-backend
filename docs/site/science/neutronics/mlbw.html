Can we drop the matrix?

Neutronics

Reich–Moore inverts a small matrix at every energy. The multi-level Breit–Wigner formalism (MLBW, ENDF's LRF = 2) has no matrix at all. It keeps only part of the multi-level theory:

  • Elastic scattering is multi-level. The neutron amplitudes of all the levels in a spin group add before squaring, as on page 13, so same-J levels interfere.
  • Capture and fission are single-level. They are page 12's SLBW peaks, summed. Any interference between levels in those channels is neglected.

Why summing capture and fission peaks is, strictly, wrong. The Schrödinger equation is linear in the wavefunction, so the contributions of different levels to the same process add as wavefunctions (amplitudes): ψ = ψ1 + ψ2. A probability is the wavefunction squared, |ψ|² = ψ*ψ, and a cross section is a probability (per unit incident flux). So a cross section is the analogue of the wavefunction squared, not of the wavefunction, and the right order is sum first, then square: |ψ1 + ψ2|² = |ψ1|² + |ψ2|² + 2 Re(ψ1*ψ2) (page 13). Adding two cross sections squares first and sums afterwards, and loses the last term.

MLBW does it properly for elastic: it adds the levels' neutron amplitudes, then squares. For capture and fission it adds the single-level cross sections, the already-squared terms, so any interference between levels in those reactions is dropped. That is an approximation, not the physics: it is good when the levels barely overlap (Γ/D small, so the cross terms are small where either peak matters), and it is what the widget below shows MLBW missing next to Reich–Moore in capture and fission.

In ENDF-102's words, the ENDF MLBW "allows multilevel computations only for elastic; for other types of partial cross sections the formalism is single-level" (Trkov et al., 2018). It is "not a true multilevel formalism, but a limit which is valid if Γ/D is small" (§2.4.14.1): when each level's width Γ is small compared with the spacing D between levels.

  • Capture. MLBW (teal) lies exactly on the dashed SLBW sum, by construction. Reich–Moore (brown) does not.
  • Elastic, then pull the levels apart. MLBW is closer to Reich–Moore than the SLBW sum is, and the gap closes as Γ/D falls. At 2 eV spacing (Γ/D ≈ 0.5) MLBW's elastic is still off by about a quarter of the peak; at 4 eV, about a tenth.
  • Different J. All three agree in every channel: nothing to interfere.

Is it actually cheaper?

Not by much, it turns out. ENDF-102 calls Reich–Moore and MLBW "equivalent in computing time" (§2.4.15), and without fission the Reich–Moore "matrix" is a single number anyway. MLBW's old advantage was elsewhere: it lends itself to a fast analytic way of Doppler broadening (the ψ, χ functions). ENDF-102 now cautions that "the use of this formalism is NOT recommended. Computation time is no longer a valid issue for modern computers" (App. D.1.2).

Older evaluations still use it. 18 of our 51 ENDF/B-VIII.0 evaluations give their first resolved range as MLBW: Na-23, Mg-24, -25 and -26, S-32, -33 and -34, Ti-46, -47, -49 and -50, Zn-64, -66, -67, -68 and -70, and two actinides, U-234 and U-236. None gives SLBW (LRF = 1).

Deeper: the MLBW formulas

Elastic, in the amplitude-squared form (ENDF-102 App. D.1.2, eqs. D.20–D.21), summed over the spin groups:

σnn = (π/k²) Σ gJ |1 − Unn|²,   Unn = e−2iφl [ 1 + Σr iΓnr / (E′r − E − iΓr/2) ]

The sum over levels r inside U is the multi-level part: the amplitudes add before |…|². Capture and fission are page 12's SLBW formulas, summed over r, with no cross terms. There is no matrix to invert: each level adds one complex term.

Why is this so? Cheaper still?

Each step so far gave up some physics and kept the stored numbers physical: every level still has an energy, a spin and real partial widths. The last step gives up even that.
