How does nuclear data store scattering angles?

Neutronics

A scattered neutron leaves in some direction. Measured from the direction it came in, that is one angle, the scattering angle θ. Nuclear data does not store θ itself but its cosine, the scattering cosine:

μ = cos θ

θ = 0°μ = +1forward

θ = 90°μ = 0sideways

θ = 180°μ = −1backward

Why the cosine? Every direction on a sphere is equally likely exactly when every value of μ between −1 and +1 is equally likely, so "isotropic" is simply a flat line in μ.

Legendre polynomials

For a curve that depends on μ, the natural shape functions are the Legendre polynomials P0(μ), P1(μ), P2(μ), …: the pole-to-pole shapes of the spherical harmonics on the last page. Look at the shapes before any formula.

The first six Legendre shapes

An angular distribution is then a weighted sum of them,

f(μ) = ½ P0 + 3⁄2 a1 P1(μ) + 5⁄2 a2 P2(μ) + …

and instead of storing the complete angular curve point by point, we store the coefficients a1, a2, … that say how much of each Legendre shape is present. (The factors (2l+1)/2 are the ENDF convention that keeps the total probability 1 (Trkov et al., 2018); with it, a1 is the average μ.)

Elastic scattering of C-12 and U-238 from their ENDF/B-VIII.0 evaluations (Brown & others, 2018), file MF=4, in the centre-of-mass frame, at the evaluation's energy nearest each button (U-238's "2 MeV" is 1.945 MeV). The coefficients are the evaluation's own, unchanged.

Forward-peaked distributions, from heavy nuclei at high energy, need many terms: U-238 at 5 MeV carries 20. Stop too early and the partial sum wobbles, and can even dip below zero, which no probability may. Low energies and light nuclei need few.

What NJOY and ACE do with it

An evaluation may give an angular distribution as Legendre coefficients, as a table of probabilities at chosen cosines, or as both, each over its own range of energies; C-12 and U-238 use coefficients up to some energy and tables above it (Trkov et al., 2018).

A Monte Carlo code wants something quick to sample, so NJOY's ACER module converts all of them into tables (MacFarlane et al., 2017). For Legendre coefficients, its routine ptleg2 (acecm.f90) rebuilds the curve at cosines it chooses adaptively, until straight lines between them are within 0.02 % of the series; thins the points to a 0.2 % tolerance; lifts any negative value to 10−10; normalises the area to 1; and writes the cosines, the density and the cumulative probability. ACE stores that table, not the original Legendre coefficients. Turn on Show what ACE stores to see its points on the curve.

quantum neutron scattering → angular distribution → a finite representation (tabulated points or Legendre coefficients) → NJOY → ACE table → OutramMC → a sampled outgoing angle

The tables here come straight from OUTRAM PARK's own port of ACER, run on the evaluation.

Sampling the result

Sample an outgoing direction

Each press draws a random number, finds where it falls on the table's cumulative probability, and reads off one cosine μ: the neutron leaves at θ = arccos μ, and the transport continues from there. That is the step OutramMC takes at every elastic collision, and it is part of what made the paths zig-zag in the first demo.

stored nuclear data → probability distribution → random number → sampled direction → the neutron continues

Press Sample 100 more a few times: the samples crowd where the curve is high, and their average cosine closes in on a1.

We now know how nuclear data can store the shape of a scattering angular distribution, and how a Monte Carlo code can sample an outgoing direction from it.

But remember our neutron matter wave?

When we changed the neutron's momentum, its de Broglie wavelength changed (page 4), and on page 5 the whole angular pattern changed with it. The nuclear interaction is strongly dependent on the neutron's energy; the evaluation shows it too, keeping a different set of coefficients at every energy.
