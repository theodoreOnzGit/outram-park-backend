Maths 1: two numbers for a collision

Neutronics

The physics pages ended with phonons, densities of states and structure. Now the maths that turns them into numbers, built up from the one collision you already know: page 2's neutron hitting a free nucleus at rest. Given the angle, the kinematics fixes the outgoing energy: one angle gives one E′.

Describe that collision with two numbers instead of an angle and an energy:

  • How much energy did the neutron gain or lose? Measured in units of kT: β = (E′ − E)/kT. Positive is a gain, negative a loss.
  • How hard was it kicked? The momentum the neutron handed over, written as an energy in units of kT: α = (E′ + E − 2μ√(EE′))/(AkT), with μ the cosine of the scattering angle and A the nucleus's mass ratio. In fact α is exactly the recoil energy a free nucleus would take from that kick, divided by kT.

For page 2's collision the neutron loses exactly the nucleus's recoil energy, so β = −α, always. Every possible collision lands on one line in the (α, β) plane: the recoil line.

The (α, β) map

The scattering law S(α, β) (page 21) is a map of which (kick, energy-change) pairs can happen, and how likely each is. For a nucleus at rest, everything sits on the line. The next pages fill the map in, one idea at a time.

Conventions on these pages. β = (E′ − E)/kT is positive when the neutron gains energy; α measures the size of the kick. S is LEAPR's asymmetric scattering law (the NJOY2016 manual's script S); ENDF stores the symmetric S = eβ/2 times it (page 21).

Common misconception: "S(α, β) = e−β S(α, −β) is what S is."

No. That equation is detailed balance: a rule every scattering law obeys, linking gaining an energy to losing it. It does not say what S is; the material decides that.
