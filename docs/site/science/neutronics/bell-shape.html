Maths 5a: what shapes the bell curve?

Neutronics

The centre is the free atom. The bell sits at the energy a free atom would take from that kick: recoil = (kick)² / (2 × atom mass). Only the kick and the mass set it; the crystal doesn't. It is rung 3's rule that on average the bound atom still takes the free recoil.

The width is how much the atoms jiggle: roughly √(2 × recoil × jiggle energy). Three ingredients, in this order:

  1. The real temperature, thermal jiggle: hotter material jiggles more, so the bell is wider. The same physics as rungs 1½–2 and Doppler broadening.
  2. A quantum jiggle that never stops, even at absolute zero: rung 5b.
  3. The crystal's springs, which decide how much each of the first two counts: rung 5c.

Move the bell

The bell curve is centred on the free-atom recoil, set by the kick and the mass. Its width is set by an effective temperature that combines the real temperature and quantum zero-point jiggle, weighted by the crystal's vibrations. (The zero-point jiggle and the effective temperature are defined on rung 5b.)

Conventions on these pages. β = (E′ − E)/kT is positive when the neutron gains energy; α measures the size of the kick. S is LEAPR's asymmetric scattering law (the NJOY2016 manual's script S); ENDF stores the symmetric S = eβ/2 times it (page 21).

Why is this so? Even at absolute zero?

Cool the widget to 1 K: the thermal width all but vanishes. But a bound atom's bell does not shrink to a line, even at absolute zero (the grey band). Why can't the atoms ever sit still?
