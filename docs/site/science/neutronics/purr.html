PURR: make up the resonances

Neutronics

Page 18's smearing kept the average and threw away how σ fluctuates. Can we keep the fluctuations without knowing where the resonances are?

Nobody knows where any single resonance sits in the unresolved range, or how wide it is. But we do know their statistics: for each spin sequence, the mean spacing and the mean widths, and how spacings and widths scatter about those means. So NJOY's PURR does something bold: it makes up resonances that obey the statistics.

Where do the statistics come from? From the evaluation, which uses measurements where it can and nuclear models where it must. U-238's ENDF/B-VIII.0 description (Brown & others, 2018) says, for example, that its average capture width was extracted from GELINA data below 1.2 keV and projected to 75 keV with a giant-dipole-resonance model, and that its neutron strength functions were set to reproduce an optical model. The shapes of the scatter are statistical theory: "the Wigner distribution" for spacings and "chi-square distributions" for widths (MacFarlane et al., 2017).

Four steps

  1. Build one made-up ladder. For each spin sequence, put the first resonance at a random place, draw its widths from the chi-square distributions, step up by a Wigner-distributed spacing to the next one, and repeat until the energy range is full. Add up all the sequences: one plausible forest, made up but statistically right.
  2. Sample it. Pick random energies in the range, away from its ends, and compute the cross sections there. Random energies are fair because a neutron's energy loss in a collision is large compared with any resonance's width (page 17's log-steps), so "neutrons arrive at random energies that are not correlated with the resonance structure" (§23.1).
  3. Count. Bin the total cross sections: how often does each value occur? For each bin, also keep the average elastic, capture and fission of the samples that landed in it.
  4. Don't trust one ladder. Repeat with many ladders, until the sampled averages settle on the infinitely dilute values that the same parameters give directly.

Make up a ladder, sample it, count

Every number is OUTRAM PARK's port of PURR (MacFarlane et al., 2017), with NJOY's own random-number generator and seed, on U-238's unresolved parameters at 50 keV and 293.6 K. Our port's tables match NJOY2016's in every word (the nuclear-data deep dive).

The made-up ladders agree with the parameters: averaged over 64 ladders, the total cross section comes out at 12.905 b, against the 12.911 b the parameters give directly, and capture at 0.317 b against 0.315 b. The NJOY2016 manual shows the same check for U-235, as "aver" against "infd" (§23.1).

All temperatures at once

PURR evaluates each ladder at every requested temperature, at the same sample energies. So the tables for different temperatures come from the same made-up resonances, and stay consistent with each other: "all temperatures are computed simultaneously to preserve temperature correlations" (§23.2).

And for U-238?

Page 18 noted that U-238's evaluation gives its infinitely dilute cross sections directly, in File 3 (LSSF = 1). Its resonance parameters then have one job: to supply the fluctuations. "When LSSF=1, all the cross section values are divided by the corresponding infinitely dilute cross sections", so each bin holds a "dimensionless fluctuation factor", applied to the cross section in File 3 (MacFarlane et al., 2017).

Why is this so? What do we end up with?

A table of how likely each value of σ is, at each energy of the unresolved range, with the matching elastic, capture and fission values for each. The fluctuations are kept, and no individual resonance had to be known.
