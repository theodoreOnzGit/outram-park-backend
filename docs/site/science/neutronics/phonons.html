What is a phonon?

Neutronics

Back to the lattice, and to the physics before the maths. Page 21 said a bound atom exchanges energy through the lattice's vibrations, in steps. What are those vibrations, and what are the steps?

1. Atoms on springs

Each atom sits at its place in the crystal, held there by its bonds to its neighbours. For small movements a bond behaves like a spring (the "harmonic approximation"; real bonds are spring-like only for small displacements). Push one atom and its neighbours follow, so the push travels as a wave.

2. The lattice vibrates in fixed patterns

A crystal can vibrate only in a set of normal modes: patterns in which every atom moves in step, each with its own frequency, like a guitar string's harmonics. It is page 8's "only certain patterns fit" again. The longest waves are sound in the solid, the acoustic modes. With two kinds of atom in the crystal, neighbours can also move against each other, which vibrates faster: the optical modes.

3. Each mode's energy comes in particles: phonons

Light is a wave, but its energy comes in particles called photons. A lattice vibration is a wave too, and its energy comes in particles called phonons. Each normal mode is a harmonic oscillator (page 9), with equally spaced energy levels, so its energy can only change in steps of ℏω: one step is one phonon.

  • Count them like particles. A vibrating lattice is a gas of phonons. A hot lattice holds more, a cold one fewer. Adding energy creates phonons; taking it away destroys them. Any number can share one mode, since phonons are bosons.
  • They move and collide. Phonons travel through the crystal (that is how heat moves through an electrical insulator) and scatter off each other.
  • But only inside the material. A phonon is a quasiparticle: a collective motion of many atoms, which cannot leave the crystal the way a photon leaves a lamp.

Why quantum steps matter here. Atoms are about 0.1 nm apart (ångströms); nuclei are femtometres across, a hundred thousand times smaller. But size alone does not decide whether the steps matter: the step ℏω compared with the other energies does. Phonon steps run up to about 0.15 eV (quartz's spectrum, page 20); a thermal neutron has 0.0253 eV, the same order. So for a thermal neutron the steps cannot be ignored. For a MeV neutron they are negligible, which is why it sees free atoms (page 20).

One neutron meets one phonon

The neutron never hits a phonon directly: it only feels nuclei, through the short-range nuclear force. But the nuclei are tied together. When the neutron kicks one, the kick passes into the lattice's vibration, and a phonon is created. If the lattice is already vibrating, the neutron can pick up that energy, and a phonon is absorbed. Written as a collision, like page 2:

  • Creation (the neutron loses energy): neutron (E) → neutron (E′) + phonon (ℏω), with E − E′ = ℏω.
  • Absorption (the neutron gains energy): neutron (E) + phonon (ℏω) → neutron (E′), with E′ − E = ℏω.

Press Send a neutron in stage 3: the count goes up by one and the neutron's energy goes down by exactly one phonon's worth. Tick "already has a phonon" and send another: the reverse.

4. A phonon has a direction and a style

Still one phonon. Two things label it: which way its wave travels, and how long that wave is; and its polarisation, its style: how the atoms move compared with that direction.

  • Longitudinal: the atoms move back and forth along the travel direction, like sound or a pushed slinky.
  • Transverse: the atoms move across it, like a wave on a rope. In 3D there are two sideways directions, so two transverse styles; this 2D toy shows one.

5. Which phonon does the neutron make?

The neutron cannot make just any phonon. Its kick has to match one, as a resonance needs the incoming energy to match a level (pages 8 and 12). Two rules, in words:

  1. Energy and wavelength must fit. The energy the neutron loses must equal the phonon's ℏω, and the kick must fit the phonon's wavelength and direction. Each phonon pairs one wavelength with one energy, so only kicks that land on an allowed pair make a phonon. Move the energy slider in stage 5: off the curve, nothing.
  2. The style must fit the push. The neutron pushes the nucleus along its kick, so it mostly makes phonons whose atoms move along the kick. A transverse phonon running along the kick is hard to make.
Like a resonance, and not

Like a resonance: there is an event only when the neutron offers exactly what the system allows, the "allowed states" idea again. Not a resonance: nothing is absorbed, there is no compound nucleus, and no Breit–Wigner peak with a lifetime width. The neutron bounces off and leaves the phonon behind.

Deeper: crystal momentum and (Q·e)²

A phonon's direction and wavelength are its wavevector q; it carries crystal momentum ℏq. Rule 1 is conservation of energy and of crystal momentum: the neutron's momentum change ℏQ must equal ℏq up to a vector G of the crystal's reciprocal lattice: the set of wavevectors whose waves repeat exactly with the crystal's own spacing, the same periodicity behind Bragg scattering (page 21). Rule 2 is the factor (Q·e)² in the one-phonon cross section, e being the atoms' direction of motion in the phonon (Squires, 2012). In the widget, a kick longer than the shortest allowed wave is folded back by a lattice vector, and then a transverse phonon can be pushed. Both rules are cleanest for coherent scattering in a single crystal; for hydrogen, which scatters incoherently, and for polycrystals and liquids, the direction matching averages out, and only the energy match survives.
