The double whammy: unresolved resonances

Neutronics

Page 17 ended at the top of U-238's resolved range, 20 keV. Above it, two things pile up at once.

  1. The log axis bunches them. A constant spacing in energy is a shrinking gap in ln E (page 17).
  2. And there are more of them. The evaluation's average level spacing itself shrinks with energy.

U-238's unresolved range runs from 20 to 149 keV, and the evaluation gives a mean spacing D for each spin group, tabulated against energy:

Across the range every spacing shrinks by about 26 %. The neutron's arrival lifts U-239 from about 4.83 to 4.95 MeV of excitation (page 8), and the density of levels grows with excitation energy (page 9). Not every evaluation shows it: U-234's keeps each D constant across its unresolved range.

Unresolved: too close to measure, not merged

The resonances in this range have not run into each other: they are still far narrower than their spacing. What has run out is the measurement. In ENDF-102's words, the unresolved range is where "the resonances still do not actually overlap", but "experimental resolution is inadequate to determine the parameters of individual resonances" (Trkov et al., 2018).

So the evaluation stores what can still be known: averages. A mean spacing, mean widths, and how much each scatters about its mean.

The simplest treatment: smear it out

If we can't see the individual peaks, average over them. In each small stretch of energy, replace the forest by its mean. This plain average over the resonances is called the infinitely dilute cross section.

From the forest to its average

ENDF/B-VIII.0 capture, reconstructed by OUTRAM PARK's RECONR port (MacFarlane et al., 2017) at 0 K. The black average is taken over a window ±5 % wide in energy, on every reconstructed point.

In the resolved range we can check the smearing, because we have both the forest and its average. For U-238 the two sides meet: just below 20 keV the smeared forest gives 0.509 b, and just above it the evaluation's average gives 0.506 b.

Where the average comes from

An evaluation can give it in either of two ways, set by a flag, LSSF (Trkov et al., 2018):

  • LSSF = 0: computed from the averages. NJOY's RECONR computes the infinitely dilute cross section from the average parameters, "based on the SLBW approximation with interference" (MacFarlane et al., 2017): page 12's formula, the cheap rung of page 16's ladder, used where only averages are known. U-234 is evaluated this way.
  • LSSF = 1: given directly. File 3 "contains the entire dilute cross section", and the parameters are kept only for what the plain average leaves out (the next page). U-238 is evaluated this way: its description says it adopts infinitely dilute capture, inelastic, total and fission cross sections from evaluations of measured data.

The average computed from the parameters is, in the manual's words, "not temperature dependent" with the approximations used. The nuclear-data deep dive follows the LSSF flag through the code.

Deeper: why the average is not just a peak with average widths

The average of a ratio is not the ratio of the averages. A level's capture contribution goes as ΓnΓγ/Γ, and the widths scatter from level to level. So RECONR averages that whole expression over the evaluation's chi-square distributions of the widths, a fluctuation integral for each spin group (MacFarlane et al., 2017):

σx = (2π²/k²) Σl,J (gJ/D) ⟨ΓnΓx/Γ⟩

with x capture or fission, and ⟨…⟩ the average over the width distributions. Elastic adds the potential scattering and an interference term, as in page 12.

Why is this so? What did we lose?

Smearing keeps the average, but it throws away how σ fluctuates from energy to energy: the peaks and the valleys between them are gone.
