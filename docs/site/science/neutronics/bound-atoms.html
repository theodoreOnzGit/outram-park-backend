But the atoms are bound!

Neutronics

On page 3 the nuclei moved freely, each a lone atom that recoils on its own when a neutron hits it: the free-gas picture. But in a reactor the atoms are not free. They are bound: in solids such as graphite, UO2, SiC and ZrH, and in molecules such as H2O.

Does that matter? It depends on how much energy the neutron can hand over, compared with the energies the bound atoms vibrate with.

Free atom or bound atom?

Si bound in α-quartz, with its vibration energies from the quartz LEAPR deck distributed with NJOY2016 (MacFarlane et al., 2017). The springs and the ladder are drawings, not a model of quartz.

Fast neutrons see free atoms

A 2 MeV neutron can hand a Si atom up to about 270 keV (page 2's kinematics). The vibrations of Si bound in α-quartz reach only 0.152 eV. The bonds cannot hold on to that kind of energy, so the struck atom recoils as if it were free, and free-gas scattering works. Even a 100 eV neutron can hand it about 13 eV, still far above the vibration energies.

Slow neutrons see the whole lattice

Once the neutron has slowed to the energies of those vibrations, the struck atom can no longer simply recoil on its own: it is tied to its neighbours. The neutron then exchanges energy with the bound system as a whole, in vibration-sized steps, and it can gain energy from the vibrations as well as lose it. That is not free-gas scattering any more.

And a thermal neutron is right in that range: 0.0253 eV, against vibration energies up to 0.152 eV in quartz. Water is the same story with molecules: its hydrogen sits in an H2O molecule that rotates, drifts and vibrates, with a continuous spectrum up to 0.149 eV and two sharp internal vibrations at 0.205 and 0.415 eV in its ENDF/B-VIII.0 model.

The thermal-scattering data in ENDF describe exactly this: "the scattering law S(α, β) describes the binding of the scattering atom in a material" (MacFarlane et al., 2017). Each evaluation stops at an energy it states itself, above which free-atom data take over: 5.0 eV for graphite, 10.0 eV for H in H2O, 1.97 eV for H in ZrH and 5.0 eV for SiC (Brown & others, 2018).

Why it matters most for moderators

Thermal neutrons do most of their scattering in the moderator: graphite, water, ZrH. So that is where the binding matters most. The binding of the fuel (UO2) and of the TRISO layers (SiC) is real too, but it matters less.

There is a second way a crystal differs from a free gas. A thermal neutron's wavelength, about 1.8 Å (page 4), is about the spacing between atoms in a crystal. So the neutron wave can diffract off the crystal's planes of atoms: Bragg scattering, which a gas of lone atoms cannot do.
