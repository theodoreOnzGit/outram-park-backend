Maths 5b: why can't atoms sit still?

Neutronics

Hold an atom in place with springs and it is confined to a small region. Quantum mechanics says a confined particle cannot have exactly zero motion: the more tightly you pin down where it is, the more spread its momentum must have. That is page 4's uncertainty relation, which is about spreads. So even in its lowest possible state, the swing keeps a small, unavoidable jiggle.

Even the ground state, the lowest possible state, still has motion.

Each vibration mode keeps an energy of ½ℏω even at absolute zero: the bottom rung of rung 3's ladder sits half a step above the bottom of the well. This unavoidable jiggle is called zero-point motion.

Cool the swing

The consequence: the atoms jiggle more than the thermometer alone would suggest, so the big-kick bell (rung 5a) is wider, as if the material were a bit hotter. The temperature that matches this wider bell is called the effective temperature, T̄. It is always at least the real temperature.

Common misconception: "Zero-point motion comes from the third law."

The third law says that as a perfect crystal is cooled towards absolute zero, it settles into its single lowest state. Quantum mechanics adds that even this lowest state still has motion. The two fit together, but the third law does not cause the jiggle. The jiggle comes from confinement: an atom pinned in place cannot have exactly zero motion.

Common misconception: "Zero-point energy is the Fermi energy."

Both are energy left over at absolute zero, but for different reasons.

  • Zero-point energy: any confined particle, even a single atom on one spring, can't sit still. Each vibration mode keeps ½ℏω: a fraction of one phonon step.
  • Fermi energy: many electrons can't share the same state (Pauli exclusion), so they are forced to stack up into higher and higher levels; in a metal that is a large energy.
  • Phonons are bosons and can pile into the same mode, so they have zero-point energy but no Fermi-style stacking.

Same lesson, quantum mechanics forbids zero energy at 0 K; different cause.

½ℏω per mode is the harmonic oscillator's ground state, exact for the springs these pages use; real bonds deviate slightly.
