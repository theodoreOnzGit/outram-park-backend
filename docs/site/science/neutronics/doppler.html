Doppler broadening: lone atoms, warm

Neutronics

A lattice is complicated, so start with the simplest thermal case: a gas of lone atoms, each drifting freely, the nuclei of page 3.

Moving nuclei, a spread of meetings

The nuclei move with the Maxwell–Boltzmann spread of velocities. So a neutron of one speed meets them at a spread of relative speeds, and the hotter the material, the wider that spread. Page 3's demo, again:

Nuclei in thermal motion

Page 3's widget: a free gas, each velocity component drawn from a Gaussian of spread √(kT/M), with M from each nucleus's ENDF/B-VIII.0 atomic weight ratio (Brown & others, 2018) and k from CODATA 2018 (Tiesinga et al., 2021).

A resonance is sharp in the relative energy (pages 12–16). If the meetings happen at a spread of relative energies, the peak the material shows is smeared too.

We do an integration, that's all

The cross section at temperature T is "that cross section that gives the same reaction rate for stationary target nuclei as the real cross section gives for moving nuclei" (MacFarlane et al., 2017). In other words: take the 0 K cross section and average it over the spread of relative speeds. That is all NJOY's BROADR does. It takes RECONR's straight-line 0 K curve (page 6's points plus interpolation), integrates it exactly, piece by piece, against the spread, and writes a new straight-line curve at temperature T.

Deeper: the integral

With ρ the target density, v the neutron's speed and P(v′, T) the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution of target velocities (NJOY2016 manual, eq. 82):

ρ v σ(v, T) = ∫ dv′ ρ |v − v′| σ(|v − v′|) P(v′, T)

It reduces to σ(v) = σ*(v) − σ*(−v), with σ*(v) an integral of the 0 K cross section against the Gaussian kernel e−α(V−v)², α = A/kT (eq. 86). The Gaussian is the Maxwell–Boltzmann bell curve; it cuts the integral off beyond about 4/√α on either side of v.

Predict first: heat the fuel. Does U-238's 6.67 eV capture peak get taller or shorter? Wider or narrower?

Rule of thumb: the area stays the same

It is the same resonance, with the same width and the same probability, only smeared out. So a wider range of neutron energies can now meet it, and the peak gets lower and wider while the area under it stays put. That is Doppler broadening. Our BROADR port on U-238's 6.674 eV capture resonance:

The rule of thumb holds very well here, and it has a condition: the manual warns that "contrary to 'popular knowledge', the area under a resonance does not remain unchanged unless E ≫ kT/A" (§4.1). For U-238, kT/A is 1.1×10−4 eV at 293.6 K, so a resonance at 6.67 eV sits tens of thousands of times above it.

Common misconception: "The area under a Doppler-broadened resonance is exactly conserved."

It is a rule of thumb, excellent for resonances far above kT/A, which in practice is all of them. Near kT/A it fails: each resonance grows a small 1/v tail (below).

Deeper: when the area rule fails

Near kT/A, each resonance grows a small 1/v tail at low energy, and the area is no longer kept. A pure 1/v cross section is the special case that does not change at all: broadening keeps the reaction rate vσ(v), and for 1/v that is a constant. Our BROADR kernel leaves a 1/v curve unchanged to 5×10−5 between 0.01 and 10 eV, about the accuracy of drawing it with straight lines between table points. And a constant cross section, such as elastic scattering, does the opposite: it grows a 1/v tail at low energy (§4.1).

See it in the real code

The nuclear-data demo runs our RECONR and BROADR ports in your browser on U-238 between 1 and 300 eV. Slide the temperature and watch the 6.67, 20.9 and 36.7 eV capture peaks fall and widen while the area under them stays put.

It works at every energy

Doppler broadening is not only a thermal-neutron effect. The nuclei are moving at any temperature above zero, so the reaction rate is averaged over relative speeds at every neutron energy: thermal, eV, keV. That is why it matters most for resonances, at eV to keV, far above thermal: heating the material changes them even though the neutrons meeting them are not thermal.

  • It shows only where σ changes over the spread. The manual's energy scale is Em = 16kT/A, for example 0.0017 eV for U-235 at 300 K; broadening matters for features narrower than about 2√(EmE), so "for 235U at 100 eV, Doppler effects are important for features smaller than about 0.8 eV" (§4.1). A smooth cross section barely changes; a 1/v one not at all; a sharp resonance a lot.
  • The free-gas form is exact only for free atoms. At thermal energies, for atoms bound in a lattice or a molecule, it is not the right physics: that is S(α, β)'s job (pages 20–21). So it applies wherever the target can be treated as free.
  • NJOY handles each range with its own module. BROADR broadens up to the top of the resolved resonance range; in the unresolved range PURR's tables carry the temperature (page 19); in the thermal range, THERMR.

Why heating the fuel this way matters for a reactor is a later lesson.
