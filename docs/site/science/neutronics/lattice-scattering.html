What does lattice scattering depend on?

Neutronics

Page 20 asked how scattering from a bound lattice differs from free atoms. It depends on five things. Two of them you have met already:

  1. The energy transfer. How much energy the neutron gives to the material, or takes from it. That is new: on page 2 the struck nucleus was at rest, so the neutron could only lose energy. A lattice that is already vibrating can give energy to the neutron.
  2. The momentum transfer. How hard the neutron is kicked, which depends on the scattering angle (page 7's μ).

The crystal's structure: planes of atoms

A thermal neutron's wavelength, about 1.8 Å (page 4), is about the spacing between planes of atoms in a crystal. So the neutron wave can diffract: waves reflected off successive planes add up when the extra path between them is a whole number of wavelengths (Bragg's law, nλ = 2d sinθ). The longest wavelength a set of planes can diffract is twice their spacing d. This is coherent elastic scattering, NJOY's "zero-phonon term": there is "no energy loss in such scattering" (MacFarlane et al., 2017). The neutron bounces off the whole crystal, whose mass is, in effect, enormous.

Graphite's Bragg edges

ENDF/B-VIII.0 crystalline graphite (Brown & others, 2018), coherent elastic (MF=7/MT=2), through OUTRAM PARK's THERMR port. Shaded: wavelengths too long to diffract.

Graphite's evaluation lists 221 Bragg edges. Each is the energy at which the wavelength becomes short enough for one more set of planes to diffract. Below the first, at 1.82 meV, the wavelength is longer than 6.70 Å, twice the widest spacing of planes that diffract (3.35 Å), and nothing diffracts at all. The manual's rule of thumb: the first edge is "typically about 2 to 5 meV" (§7.1). Between edges the cross section falls as 1/E; above about an electronvolt the steps have shrunk away.

But we need thermal effects too

  1. The temperature. The atoms are already vibrating, more so when hot, and that changes how much energy the neutron can give or receive. Switch the widget to 1200 K: the edges stay where they are, set by the plane spacings, but the steps shrink, from 3.20 b to 1.27 b at 0.1 eV. A crystal that shakes more diffracts less cleanly.
  2. The vibration fingerprint. The atoms do not vibrate as free particles: they are tied to each other, so the lattice vibrates together, in collective patterns. A bound atom can only exchange energy through those patterns, in steps of a quantum of vibration (a phonon, page 23). Each pattern's steps are quantised, but a real crystal has a continuous spread of patterns, and its spectrum of vibration frequencies ρ is the material's fingerprint. Page 20 read two of them from their decks: Si in quartz, and H in water.

One table holds all of it: S(α, β)

All five sit in one function of two numbers, the scattering law S(α, β): α measures the momentum transfer and β the energy transfer, both scaled by kT. The structure, the temperature and the vibrations are all in S; the evaluation stores it as a table. For the inelastic part, in ENDF's form (NJOY2016 manual, eq. 230):

σ(E → E′, μ) = (σb/2kT) √(E′/E) e−β/2 S(α, β)

Deeper: α, β and detailed balance

From the NJOY2016 manual, §7.2 (eqs. 231–232), with A the scatterer's mass ratio and μ the laboratory scattering cosine:

β = (E′ − E)/kT,   α = (E′ + E − 2μ√(EE′))/(AkT)

The S in the formula above is ENDF's symmetric S, which is why the factor e−β/2 appears; LEAPR's own asymmetric form folds it in. Symmetric means S(α, β) = S(α, −β): with the e−β/2 factor, a neutron gains a given energy less often than it loses it, by e−β. That is detailed balance, the condition that lets neutrons come into thermal equilibrium with the material.

The Bragg steps also depend on temperature: the more the atoms shake about their places, the less cleanly the planes reflect. The factor that measures this weakening is called the Debye–Waller factor, e−2WEi in the manual's coherent elastic formula (eq. 225). So "structure first, temperature later" is the teaching order, not a physical separation.

Common misconception: "S(α, β) = e−β S(α, −β) is what S is."

No. That equation (in LEAPR's asymmetric form) is detailed balance: a rule every scattering law obeys, linking gaining energy to losing it. It does not tell you S; S itself depends on the material's structure, temperature and vibrations.

Three kinds of thermal scattering

  • Coherent elastic: no energy change, Bragg diffraction off the crystal's planes. Crystals such as graphite and beryllium.
  • Incoherent elastic: no energy change, but no Bragg edges. Hydrogenous solids such as ZrH.
  • Inelastic: the neutron gains or loses energy by exciting or absorbing vibrations. Every material.

Which kinds an evaluation carries is in its data. Of the local ENDF/B-VIII.0 tapes, graphite and SiC have coherent elastic data and Bragg edges; H in H2O has none: water is a liquid, with no planes to diffract from.

Why is this so? Start with the simplest case

Thermal effects come first. But a lattice is complicated, so let's break it down, and think about a simpler case first: lone atoms.
