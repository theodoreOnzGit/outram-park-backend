Maths 3: one swing

Neutronics

Tie the atom to a spring, one vibration mode (page 23). Picture a child on a swing whose energy can only go up or down in whole steps of ℏω: phonons.

The neutron gives the swing one sudden shove. Afterwards there is some chance it has gained 0 steps, some chance 1, some chance 2, and so on. On average the shove adds just enough steps to carry the free-recoil energy (rung 2's sum rule). So:

  • a gentle shove usually adds nothing: the neutron bounces off with its energy unchanged, elastic scattering;
  • a hard shove adds many steps, and their spread takes on a familiar bell shape.

Shove the swing

How can a gentle kick make a whole phonon?

The kick decides how likely a phonon is. The neutron's own energy decides whether a phonon is possible at all. When a phonon is made, the neutron pays for it in full.

  • Case A: creating a phonon means down-scatter. The neutron has plenty of energy (E > ℏω) but only grazes the atom, so the free recoil is, say, a tenth of a step. It can still make one phonon, sometimes: roughly 1 collision in 10 does, and leaves with E′ = E − ℏω; the rest make none. Energy is conserved in every collision: the neutron never loses "a tenth of a step". The tenth is the average over many collisions, not a budget for each one.
  • Case B: creation is impossible. The neutron has less than one step of energy (E < ℏω): there is nothing to pay for a phonon with. It can still bounce off elastically, or, if the swing is warm, take a phonon that is already there and leave faster. In a cold lattice there is almost nothing to take, so elastic is all that is left.
Common misconception: "A gentle kick can't make a whole phonon."

It can, sometimes. The neutron then pays the full ℏω from its own energy (Case A); the gentle kick only makes it rare.

Up-scatter and down-scatter

Here the two words get their meaning. Down-scatter: the neutron loses energy by making a phonon. Up-scatter: it gains energy by absorbing a phonon that already exists. Up-scatter needs a warm lattice, because a cold one holds almost no phonons to give away; the hotter, the more likely. It is open to any neutron in a warm lattice, not only slow ones. Still, a neutron is always more likely to lose a given amount of energy than to gain it: the ratio is detailed balance.

Deeper: detailed balance as a formula

In the asymmetric convention used here: S(α, β) = e−β S(α, −β). Gaining an energy β (in kT) is e−β times as likely as losing it.

It is why thermal neutrons settle into a spectrum at the moderator's temperature instead of slowing down forever. (Spectrum calculations come later.)

Putting it together: the phonon expansion

We now know everything a single collision with our swing can do. Sort the possibilities by how many phonons are made:

  • 0 phonons: the neutron bounces off with the same energy (elastic);
  • 1 phonon: it loses exactly one step (or gains one, if the swing was already moving: up-scatter);
  • 2 phonons: two steps; and so on.

Each case has its own chance, the bars above, and its own energy change, a whole number of steps. To describe everything that can happen, write each case with its chance and add them all up:

everything = (chance of 0) × [no change] + (chance of 1) × [one step] + (chance of 2) × [two steps] + …

Writing the answer as a sum of "0 phonons + 1 phonon + 2 phonons + …" is called the phonon expansion. "Expansion" is the mathematician's word for writing something as a sum of simpler pieces. The bars in the widget are the phonon expansion for one swing, one bar per term.

Where does Poisson come from?

A sudden shove is written as an exponential: the swing's response contains exp(i × kick × displacement). Writing it out term by term, exp(x) = 1 + x + x²/2! + …, sorts the outcomes by phonon count: 1 is no phonon, x one phonon, x²/2! two. Squared, this gives the chances e−n̄ n̄n/n!, with n̄ the free recoil counted in steps: a Poisson distribution. The exponential comes from the physics of the kick; the phonon counts come out of expanding it. The NJOY2016 manual says the same: "expanding the time-dependent part of the exponential" (§24, phonon expansion).

Rare events, the bell shape, and a warm swing

Rare events, as intuition only. Poisson is the law of many independent chances, each unlikely; picture the kick as many tiny chances to make a phonon. The exact reason is the expansion above, not literally independent trials.

Poisson and the bell curve. A Poisson distribution with a large mean looks like a bell centred at the mean, with width √mean. That is why a hard kick's bars take a bell shape. For a small mean it is lopsided, mostly 0 and a little 1: not a bell at all. Try both with the kick slider.

A warm swing. If the swing is already moving, the neutron can also absorb phonons, and the chances become a two-sided version of Poisson, written with modified Bessel functions: the manual's eq. 542, which the widget computes. The mean stays the free recoil.

Conventions on these pages. β = (E′ − E)/kT is positive when the neutron gains energy; α measures the size of the kick. S is LEAPR's asymmetric scattering law (the NJOY2016 manual's script S); ENDF stores the symmetric S = eβ/2 times it (page 21).
