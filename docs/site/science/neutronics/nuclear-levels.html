Why are nuclear energy levels so complicated?

Neutronics

The box on the last page had hard walls and evenly growing levels. A nucleus is more interesting. Here is the picture built up one honest step at a time; each step says what it fixes and what it still gets wrong.

Softer walls: the harmonic oscillator

A nucleon near the centre of a nucleus feels little force; one near the edge is pulled back. A slightly better analogy than the box is the harmonic oscillator, whose walls are soft. Its levels are equally spaced, and they come in groups: shell N holds (N + 1)(N + 2) protons or neutrons. When a group is full, the shell closes, at 2, 8, 20, 40, 70, … nucleons (Krane, 1987).

Better, but still not quite. The oscillator's walls keep rising forever; a real nucleus has an edge.

The nuclear potential

A realistic shape is the Woods–Saxon potential: flat across the inside of the nucleus, with a soft edge about the size of the nucleus (Woods & Saxon, 1954). Neutrons and protons feel slightly different potentials; protons also repel each other through the Coulomb force.

Magic numbers

Nuclei with 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82 or 126 protons or neutrons are especially stable: the magic numbers. The oscillator gets the first three right and misses the rest (it predicts 40 and 70, which are not special).

Spin–orbit coupling

A nucleon spins, and it also orbits. Whether its spin lines up with its orbital motion or against it changes its energy, and in a nucleus that change is large: each level splits in two. The piece with the larger total angular momentum drops into the shell below. With that one addition the gaps fall exactly at the magic numbers (Mayer, 1949) (Haxel et al., 1949). Pairing

Like nucleons pair up, two neutrons or two protons, and a pair is bound more tightly than two loners. So nuclei with even numbers of both protons and neutrons are extra tightly bound (Krane, 1987).

Predict. A neutron joins U-235, making U-236 (92 protons, 144 neutrons: both even). Another joins U-238, making U-239 (147 neutrons: odd). Which gives off more energy as it binds? The first: its new neutron completes a pair. That is part of why slow neutrons can split U-235 and not U-238.

Many nucleons moving together

In heavy nuclei, many nucleons can also move together: the whole nucleus can vibrate in fixed modes, or, if it is deformed, rotate (Bohr & Mottelson, 1998). So an excited state can be one nucleon lifted to a higher orbit (single-particle), or many nucleons moving together (collective vibrations and rotations).

At the energy a captured neutron brings, several MeV above the ground state, all of these combine. The number of levels per unit of energy, the level density, climbs steeply with the excitation energy and with the number of nucleons (Bohr & Mottelson, 1998). It is very complex, and that is the point: these are states of the whole compound nucleus, not single-particle levels.

ENDF/B-VIII.0 evaluations (Brown & others, 2018), total cross section reconstructed at 0 K by OUTRAM PARK's own RECONR port (MacFarlane et al., 2017). Thinned for a phone: 900 steps of log E, keeping the highest and the lowest point of each step, so peaks and troughs survive; nothing is smoothed.

  • He-4 is nearly smooth: no resonance parameters at all on its tape, and one broad bump, highest near 1.1 MeV (7.6 b at 1.125 MeV). He-4 is doubly magic, and He-5, which a neutron would make, is not even bound.
  • Mn-55 has a handful of large, well-separated resonances: its evaluation lists 187 resolved resonances up to 125 keV.
  • U-238 has thousands of narrow, crowded ones: 3 345 resolved resonances below 20 keV (926 with l = 0 and 2 419 with l = 1), and more, unresolved, up to 149 keV.
