What can happen when a neutron meets a nucleus?

Neutronics

Page 9 showed that nuclear levels are complicated. So let's simplify: what can actually happen? Think of the neutron joining the nucleus to make a compound nucleus, which then leaves by one of a few exits. Here they are for the energies a reactor cares about, from fission energies (about 2 MeV) down to thermal.

The compound nucleus and its exits

Every Q-value here is the evaluation's own, from the ENDF/B-VIII.0 tapes (Brown & others, 2018); the MT numbers are those of the ENDF-6 format (Trkov et al., 2018).

Predict first. A 2 MeV neutron hits U-238. Which exits are open? Elastic, inelastic, capture and fission; (n,2n) is closed.

1. Elastic scattering, (n,n), MT 2

The neutron leaves with the same total kinetic energy in the centre-of-mass frame, and the nucleus stays in its ground state: page 2's collision. Not all of it goes through a compound nucleus: away from resonances, most elastic scattering is potential scattering, the wave bending round the nucleus of page 5.

2. Inelastic scattering, (n,n′), MT 51–91, total MT 4

The neutron leaves the nucleus in an excited state, and comes out with less energy; the nucleus then gives off a gamma ray. The prime on n′ marks the excited nucleus left behind; (n,n) and (n,n′) are both scattering. Inelastic scattering is a threshold reaction: the neutron must bring at least the first excited level's energy (a little more in the laboratory frame).

Here page 9 pays off. Heavy, deformed nuclei have low-lying collective levels, rotations and vibrations, so their inelastic scattering opens at low energy. Choose U-238 and C-12 above and compare the thresholds: U-238's first level is about 45 keV up, C-12's about 4.4 MeV, a factor of about 100. That is why inelastic scattering matters for slowing fission neutrons in uranium, and barely at all in graphite at those energies.

3. Radiative capture, (n,γ), MT 102

The neutron stays, and the compound nucleus gets rid of its excitation as gamma rays. Even a neutron captured at rest releases energy: its binding energy, a few MeV. A rock falling to Earth hits the ground with the energy the attraction gave it; a captured neutron "lands" on the nucleus in the same way, except that the nuclear force is far stronger. One honest caveat: gravity pulls from far away, but the nuclear force is short-range, a few femtometres, so a neutral neutron feels nothing until it is all but touching the nucleus. There is no long fall from outer space; what the analogy captures is the energy released on landing.

In a reactor, capture that does not cause fission removes a neutron from the chain reaction: it is called parasitic capture.

4. (n,p), MT 103, and (n,α), MT 107

The compound nucleus can instead throw out a charged particle. Whether that exit exists depends on its Q-value. For B-10, (n,α) is exothermic, open at any energy, which is why boron is a control absorber; for C-12 it needs several MeV. The same picture covers both.

5. (n,2n), MT 16

The neutron knocks out a second neutron: one in, two out. It is not scattering, though neutrons come out: it leaves a different nuclide, with one neutron fewer (A − 1), and two neutrons. ENDF files it as its own reaction, MT 16, among the non-elastic reactions, not under inelastic scattering, which is exactly the one-neutron-out channels, MT 4 = MT 51–91 (Trkov et al., 2018). It needs at least the energy to pull a neutron out of the target, about 6 MeV for U-238, so it multiplies neutrons only for the fastest fission neutrons.

6. Fission, MT 18

And for uranium, the compound nucleus can split. That deserves a page of its own.

Every reaction is the compound nucleus, or the potential, choosing an exit, and the Q-value decides which exits exist at a given energy. Nuclear data stores a cross section for each exit, which is why ENDF gives each one an MT number.
