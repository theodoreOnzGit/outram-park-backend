At a collision: who, what, where to?

Transport

The neutron has arrived at a collision site with energy E. Three questions follow, in order, and each is answered by one or two random numbers.

1. Which nuclide?

A material is a mixture. The neutron hit nuclide i with probability proportional to its share of the macroscopic total cross section, Niσt,i(E)/Σt (Material::sample_nuclide; in the unresolved range, sample_nuclide_urr, which weights by the probability-table band the flight already used, as OpenMC does). One uniform number ξ1 is multiplied by Σt and compared with the running sum.

In Godiva's uranium at 1 MeV (our port's data, 293.6 K) U-235 takes 93.6 % of the collisions and U-238 5.3 %; at 0.1 MeV U-235 takes 94.0 %. The shares follow the atom densities, the cross sections being close to each other here (each is of order 7 b at 1 MeV).

2. Which reaction?

Now a second number ξ2, against the chosen nuclide's own total, and a ladder of cumulative partial cross sections, always in the same order: fission first, then the rest of absorption (capture), inelastic scattering, (n,2n), other channels, and elastic last (transport_csg.rs, the collision block). One ladder serves every case; the variance-reduction option of survival biasing offsets the draw past the absorption rungs instead of duplicating it.

For U-235 at 1 MeV the partial cross sections are, in barns: fission 1.2029, capture 0.1073, inelastic 1.7207, elastic 3.8726. Elastic scattering, the last rung, is the widest.

Predict first: in a bare sphere of highly enriched uranium, which is the most likely thing to happen at a fast collision: fission, capture, or a scatter? Move the energy to 2 MeV: what changes?

3. Where to? What each reaction does

  • Capture ends the history (an absorption event on the track).
  • Fission ends the neutron's history too, and banks the sites of the neutrons it makes: the fission bank is page 9.
  • Elastic scattering samples the scattering angle in the centre-of-mass frame from the evaluation's own distribution (pages 6 and 7 of the neutronics lessons, How are scattering angles stored?), and then, depending on the energy and on the nuclide, one of three kernels takes it to the laboratory frame (the fork scatter_fork in transport_csg.rs):
    • below the cutoff of a bound-atom table the nuclide carries (hydrogen in water, carbon in graphite): the thermal table's S(α,β) law gives the outgoing energy and angle directly, up-scattering included (page 45 of the neutronics lessons);
    • otherwise, in the free-gas range, a target velocity is sampled and the collision is done with that moving nucleus, with the resonance rejection of DBRC for heavy nuclides (page 42 and page 43);
    • above 400 kT (above 1 keV for a nuclide with a DBRC table), the target is taken at rest, and the collision is the textbook two-body one of page 2.
  • Inelastic scattering leaves the nucleus excited: a discrete level uses the level's own angular distribution (and isotropic only if the evaluation has none), and the continuum uses the evaluated energy-angle law (sample_inelastic).
  • (n,2n) and the higher multiplicities emit two or more neutrons, drawn independently from the evaluated law; copying the first neutron's state would correlate the pair perfectly (that was a defect, gh:#192).

Whatever the outcome, the cross sections used are the collision material's own at its own temperature (not a run-wide one), so a model with fuel, moderator and reflector at different temperatures partitions each collision correctly (mat_temp = material.temperature). The full chapter, with the code, is Collisions, fission and delayed neutrons.
