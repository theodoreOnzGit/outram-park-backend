Surfaces: where the flight ends

Transport

The flight of page 1 was sampled as if the medium went on forever. It does not. At every point the neutron is inside a cell of the geometry, a region made of one material and bounded by surfaces (planes, spheres, cylinders). So the code asks two questions at the start of each flight:

  1. distance to collision: dcol = −ln ξ/Σt, from the material of the cell the neutron is in;
  2. distance to boundary: db, along the neutron's direction, to the nearest surface of the cell, over every level of nested geometry (distance_to_boundary).

If dcol < db the neutron moves dcol and collides. Otherwise it moves db to the surface, the boundary condition of that surface is applied, and the neutron starts a new flight in whatever cell it is now in. Because the flight is memoryless (page 1), the new flight is simply drawn again: nothing is lost by stopping at the surface. This loop is steps 2 to 6 of the module doc of transport_csg.rs, ported in structure from OpenMC's history-based transport (Romano et al., 2015).

Boundary conditions

What happens at a surface is a property of the surface, chosen by whoever built the model (Geometry::cross_surface, crossing/mod.rs):

BoundaryWhat happens to the neutronUsed for
Transmissive (an ordinary interface)passes through to the cell on the far side; a new flight starts therethe inside of the model: fuel to cladding, pebble to coolant
Vacuumthe neutron is killed and counted as leakagethe outside of a bare assembly such as Godiva
Reflectivespecular reflection; at a corner the reflections off every reflective wall meeting there are composed, so the neutron leaves the corner instead of staying in ita symmetry plane, or the wall of a repeated cell: a single pebble in its reflective cell stands for an infinite array
Whitere-emitted with a cosine-law (diffuse) angular distributiona wall that returns neutrons with an isotropic (cosine-law) distribution
Periodicrefused: not implemented, so the code stops with a message (GitHub #259)it needs a partner surface; reflective is not a substitute

Until 2026-09-22 white and periodic fell through to a specular reflection and returned a plausible k for a different problem; that was corrected by refusing the one and implementing the other (the comment in cross_surface keeps the old claim struck through).

Standing on a surface is the hard part

After the neutron moves db it sits on the surface, to within rounding. Which cell is it in now? The obvious answer is to put the point into the surface's equation (for a sphere, f = |r|2 − R2) and read the sign. But on the surface f is zero in exact arithmetic, and in floating point it is whatever rounding left. If the sign is wrong the neutron is put back in the cell it was leaving; the next distance-to-boundary then ignores the surface it is standing on, finds nothing ahead, and the neutron streams out of the problem. A tracking error reads exactly like leakage.

Predict first: a neutron enters a sphere, so it is inside. If you decide its side by the sign of the surface equation at the point it was moved to, how often are you right at head-on incidence? At grazing incidence?

Which side is the neutron on?

Doubles, as the transport code uses (IEEE 754 binary64, identical in JavaScript and Rust). Each of 20 000 random rays is stepped onto a unit sphere by the flight distance. The first bar decides the side by the sign of the surface equation; the second uses the recorded side; the third nudges the point 1e−9 cm along the normal first. The check on this page prints the result at four impact parameters: 0 % head on (where f is exactly zero and reads as not-inside), 100 % at b = 0.5, about 93 % near grazing. The sign is not evidence of the side, and the two fixes are right every time. JavaScript sketch; the real code is nudge_across in crossing/mod.rs.

The code does two independent things about it (geometry/crossing/mod.rs):

  1. Record the side; do not re-derive it. At the crossing the outgoing side is known exactly: it is the sign of u·n, the direction against the surface normal. The crossing returns it as a signed surface token and locate trusts it. OpenMC does the same with its signed surface index.
  2. Nudge along the normal. As a second guard, which OpenMC does not have, the neutron is moved 10−9 cm across the surface along the normal (nudge_across), so the equation changes sign however tangent the direction is.

The case that made both necessary (GitHub #168): on a pebble made of concentric shells with a reflective outer wall, where physical leakage is zero, the tracker lost 85 % of the source neutrons. The regression test reflective_concentric_shells_do_not_leak records, measured 2026-09-10: with both fixes disabled the geometry leaks 0.0860 per source neutron (k = 2.03432 ± 0.01644); with both in place it leaks exactly 0.0 (k = 2.22599 ± 0.01072) (crossing/tests.rs:285). The bug moved k by about 9 % and gave a perfectly plausible number; nothing crashed.

Common misconception: "a geometry bug shows up as an error message."

It shows up as a believable change in leakage or k. The only defence is a test whose right answer is known exactly, such as zero leakage from a fully reflective model.

Nested geometry has its own frame

A TRISO particle inside a pebble inside a core is a stack of universes, and each universe's surfaces are defined about its own origin. A sphere's normal evaluated at the global position points about the wrong centre, and the nudge then pushes the neutron the wrong way. cross_surface_in_frame evaluates the crossing in the universe's own frame. The doc comment records the measurement that found it: on a 3×3×3 TRISO lattice (2026-09-14), 37.4 % of kernel-sphere crossings assigned the next flight to the wrong cell, against 0.0 % of crossings of root-level planes (crossing/mod.rs:388). The full story, with the code, is in the Monte Carlo deep dive, Surface tracking and the leak that was not physics.
