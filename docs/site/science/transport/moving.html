Moving to the collision

Transport

The last page gave a distance d. Moving the neutron is the easy part:

r ← r + d u

where u is the neutron's direction, a unit vector. The code does exactly this (r = stream(r, u, d_col), transport_csg.rs), and advances the neutron's clock by the time of flight d/v, with v the speed for the neutron's energy (flight_time: non-relativistic, which the source comment puts 1.1 % low in speed at 20 MeV and negligible below). A flight does not change the neutron's energy or direction; a collision does, and that is page 5.

Where the direction comes from

A neutron is born at a fission with a direction drawn isotropically, every direction equally likely (isotropic_direction), and an energy drawn from the fissioning nuclide's own fission spectrum. It then flies in a straight line until a collision gives it a new direction. In the first generation of a run there is no fission yet, so the source is a box of points rejected into fissionable material; fission sites replace it from then on (page 9).

What gets recorded on the way

Each straight piece is a segment. The standard way to estimate a flux or a reaction rate is to add, for each segment, the neutron's weight times its length to the cell and energy bin it crossed: the track-length estimator (score_track_length). Under surface tracking the code scores flux and reaction rates this way (the collision estimator is used instead inside delta-tracked regions, page 8).

Predict first: in the demo below, count the dots on one neutron's path. How many collisions does a fast neutron in a uranium sphere typically make before something ends its history? What are the ways it can end?

This is OUTRAM PARK's own transport code running in your browser (the Monte Carlo demo of the dhoby-ghaut crate, compiled to WebAssembly) on ENDF/B-VIII.0 nuclear data (Brown & others, 2018) that your browser downloads and processes first; allow about a minute the first time. It opens on recorded tracks, labelled as recorded, and switches to live ones when the data are ready. The page stays responsive meanwhile.

What to look at: each straight line is a flight; each corner is a collision where the neutron changes direction (and, as you will see, energy); a history ends in a capture, a fission or a leak through the sphere's surface. Those are the events the code records on a track: born, surface crossing, scatter, fission, absorption, leak, and, for runs with variance reduction, roulette kills (TrackEvent). One more, Lost, means the neutron was lost by the tracker, and a track that ends there is a defect report and not a history. Page 3 is about how that can happen.
