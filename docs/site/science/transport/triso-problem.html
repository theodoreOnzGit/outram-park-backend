TRISO: too many surfaces

Transport

Surface tracking (page 3) is exact and simple, and it is slow where a geometry has very many surfaces. HTR-10 fuel is the extreme case.

What a TRISO particle is

A TRISO particle is a grain of uranium oxide, the kernel, about half a millimetre across, wrapped in four coatings that keep the fission products in. In the HTR-10 the kernel has a radius of 250 µm and is surrounded by a porous carbon buffer (90 µm), an inner pyrolytic carbon layer (40 µm), silicon carbide (35 µm) and an outer pyrolytic carbon layer (40 µm): five concentric spheres per particle (Li et al., 2014). A fuel pebble is a 6 cm graphite ball whose inner 5 cm hold 8335 of them in graphite matrix, and the core is a heap of such pebbles (a core model in the records has 16 681 balls) (numbers as recorded in the Monte Carlo tutorial, rung 5).

Predict first: a neutron crosses one fuel zone and meets a few of these particles on its way. How many surfaces does the tracker stop it at before the neutron's first collision?

Why surface tracking struggles

Two things multiply. At every flight, distance_to_boundary takes the nearest surface over every nesting level, so inside the fuel zone it returns the next TRISO shell. And every shell it reaches stops the neutron, which then asks where it is (locate) and starts a new flight. Since the kernel is tiny and the carbon around it is nearly transparent, most stops are not collisions.

The measurement, in the tutorial's delta-tracking demo, whose cell is the same two-dimensional TRISO analogue (particles as rods; a flat universe of 763 cells; 1000 neutrons × [10 inactive + 40 active], 2026-10-07, Loose data tier; Intel i9-13900K, 16 logical cores, 62 GB, Linux, CPU only, one thread per run, shared with other builds): surface tracking made 1201 stops per neutron, 1096 of them crossings, against only 105 real collisions, and took 6.6 ms per neutron. (Quote the ratio for this model only: a surface tracker with a lattice or a grid could close some of the gap, as the tutorial says.)

This is OUTRAM PARK's own transport code running in your browser (the Monte Carlo demo of the dhoby-ghaut crate, compiled to WebAssembly) on ENDF/B-VIII.0 nuclear data (Brown & others, 2018) that your browser downloads and processes first; allow about a minute the first time. It opens on recorded tracks, labelled as recorded, and switches to live ones when the data are ready. The page stays responsive meanwhile.

Zoom into one of the specks in the demo: it is a TRISO particle drawn as a rod, and the orange dot at its centre is the kernel. Count the track's segments near the fuel: most are tiny hops across shells. (The demo is the 2D analogue, with five shells per particle, as above.)

Next question

The neutron does not need to know about every shell. It only needs to know what it is made of where it collides. Can we stop asking where the surfaces are?
