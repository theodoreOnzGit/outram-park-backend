Repeat: one neutron, birth to death

Transport

Put the pages together. A history is this loop, run until something ends it (transport_history_vr in transport_csg.rs):

  1. Locate the neutron: which cell, which material (Geometry::locate); Σt from that material at the neutron's energy.
  2. Sample the flight, dcol = −ln ξ/Σt (page 1), and find db, the distance to the nearest surface (page 3).
  3. If dcol < db: move there (page 2) and handle the collision (page 5): draw the nuclide, then the reaction. A scatter changes the energy and direction and the loop goes back to step 1; capture and fission end the history.
  4. Otherwise move to the surface and apply its boundary condition: pass into the next cell, reflect (back to step 1), or leak (the history ends).

That is all a history is. Everything else in a transport code is bookkeeping around it, the choice of where the neutrons start (page 9), or a way to make step 2 cheaper (page 8).

Predict first: in a bare uranium sphere, what is the most common end of a history: capture, fission or leak? In a graphite-moderated pebble, where the neutron must first slow down?

Things the picture tells you without a number: in Godiva the paths are straight and fast and the neutron rarely loses much energy per collision, because uranium is heavy (page 2 of the neutronics lessons); in graphite it loses energy in small steps, and slowing down takes many collisions. The collision strip of the demo's cross-section panel draws the energy after each collision.

What a track records

For each history the code can keep a track: the position, direction, energy, time and weight at the birth and after every event, with the kind of event. The kinds are born, surface crossing, scatter, fission, absorption, rouletted, leak, lost (TrackEvent). The demos draw those tracks; the numbers in their panels (scatters, crossings, path length, time of flight, minimum energy) are computed from them (History::from_track in the demo's history.rs).
