Delta tracking: stop looking at surfaces

Transport

Delta tracking was introduced by Woodcock and co-workers in the 1960s for the GEM code (Woodcock et al., ANL-7050, 1965) and is the standard way Serpent moves neutrons through complicated geometry (Leppänen, 2010). The idea fits in four lines.

The idea

Choose a majorant Σmaj(E) that is at least as large as the total cross section of every material the neutron could be in. Then:

  1. sample the flight on the majorant, s = −ln ξ1/Σmaj (page 1), and move there without looking for any surface;
  2. look up the material at the landing point (a point query: which cell am I in?) and its true Σt;
  3. with probability Σt/Σmaj the collision is real: handle it as on page 5. Otherwise it is virtual: nothing happens, and the flight simply continues from there.

A virtual collision is a fictitious reaction that makes the medium look uniform: a region with a smaller Σt is topped up to Σmaj with collisions that change nothing. The real collisions then have exactly the distribution surface tracking would give (this is the theorem; the page's widget and check confirm it numerically), so the method is unbiased for any valid majorant. A loose majorant costs only time, more virtual collisions; the neutron never has to find a surface.

Predict first: in a slab with a thin strong absorber next to a thick weak medium, delta tracking flies on the strong value everywhere. Which costs more flights per neutron, and does the distribution of real collisions change?

Two ways to the same collisions

A one-dimensional sketch of the algorithm of physics/delta_tracking/flight.rs: two regions, a source at the left, vacuum at the right, 20 000 neutrons each way. The two histograms estimate the same distribution; the check on this page compares both with the closed-form fractions (collision in A, in B, leak) to within four standard errors. The text below the plot counts the flights, boundary stops and virtual collisions. The real code, on real geometry, is the demo further down.

Our implementation

  • New work, not a port. OpenMC is pure surface tracking; a search of its tree for "delta tracking", "woodcock" and "majorant" finds nothing (delta_tracking/mod.rs). The code is therefore verified differently: against surface tracking on the same problem.
  • Per region. A cell is declared delta-tracked (Cell::delta_tracked, TrackingMethod::Delta) with its own majorant; everything else stays surface-tracked. It matters, because one strong absorber raises the majorant everywhere it is used: adding one B4C control rod to a globally bounded material set cost 26.3× in tracking steps at the thermal peak (examples/majorant_absorber_price.rs, 2026-09-17).
  • The majorant is energy-dependent and must bound. Majorant::bounding tabulates Σmaj(E) on every nuclide's own energy points, with a 10 % margin in the demos. A majorant that is too small is the dangerous direction. On ENDF/B-VIII.0 an earlier version sampled bins and left the HTR-10 kernel 18 % above the majorant at 1.689 MeV, which is why it is a bound by construction now (gh:#585).
  • It still stops at the nearest surface. Inside a delta region each flight is sampled on the majorant but the neutron advances at most to the nearest surface (handoff, gh:#599); a collision beyond it is discarded and re-drawn from the surface, which is exact because the flight is memoryless. So internal surfaces are not skipped; the cost on a three-shell sphere was measured at 2.5× the wall time of surface tracking, at equal k (gh:#599 audit, 2026-10-06, i9-13900K, one thread, machine loaded).
  • Tallies change. There is no single material along a delta flight, so the track-length estimator would attribute the path to the wrong cross sections; the code scores at the real collision point (score_collision_point). Scoring the start-of-flight material put the tallied k∞ of the HTR-10 bed 4175 pcm below what the run's own balance implies, until the collision estimator was used (as Serpent does in such regions (Leppänen, 2017)).

What was measured

Timings carry their hardware: Intel i9-13900K, 16 logical cores, 62 GB, Linux, CPU only, one thread per run, shared with other builds, as recorded in the tutorial (the ratios are meaningful on that one machine only). All of these are quoted from the Monte Carlo tutorial's rung 5 (the delta-tracking demo's cell, the 2D TRISO analogue, 1000 neutrons × [10 + 40] per method, seed 784, 2026-10-07); they were not re-run for this page.

See it in the real code

The delta-tracking demo runs the same neutron through the same 2D TRISO cell both ways in your browser, one step per tap, and "Run many" gives k∞ by both methods and the cost per history; it has a "majorant too low" arm.

This is OUTRAM PARK's own transport code running in your browser (the Monte Carlo demo of the dhoby-ghaut crate, compiled to WebAssembly) on ENDF/B-VIII.0 nuclear data (Brown & others, 2018) that your browser downloads and processes first; allow about a minute the first time. This demo processes the data and builds the majorant first, which takes a minute or two. The page stays responsive meanwhile.
