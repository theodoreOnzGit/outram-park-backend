Nothing happens between collisions

A neutron with no electric charge feels nothing as it flies. It goes in a straight line until it hits a nucleus. So the first question of any transport code is: how far does it go?

Describe the medium by its macroscopic cross section Σt (units 1/cm): the chance, per centimetre flown, that the neutron collides. It is the sum over the nuclides in the material of their number density times their microscopic cross section at the neutron's energy, Σt = ∑i Niσt,i(E) (Material::macro_xs_total, material.rs:140). If the neutron has survived to distance x, the chance it survives a further dx is 1 − Σtdx, so the survival probability is

P(no collision in x) = e−Σtx

and the density of the distance d to the first collision is Σte−Σtd, with mean 1/Σt, the mean free path.

Predict first: what fraction of flights are shorter than one mean free path? Longer than three?

One random number per flight

To draw d from that density the code uses the inverse-transform trick: if ξ is uniform on (0, 1), then

d = −ln(ξ) / Σt

has exactly the law above (the survival probability is uniform, so set it equal to ξ and solve). In the transport loop it is one line (transport_csg.rs, the surface_arm): d = -xi.max(f64::MIN_POSITIVE).ln() / sigma_t. The max keeps ξ off zero so the logarithm is finite. In a void, Σt = 0 and the flight is infinite: the neutron goes straight to the next boundary.

Sampling the flight

JavaScript only, on purpose: the flight is the single line above and the real code adds nothing to see. The bars are 30 000 draws of −ln ξ/Σ; the curve is the exact density; the dashed line is the mean free path. The page-2 demo runs the real code. A check on this page (check-transport.js) compares a million-draw mean, and the fractions beyond one and three mean free paths, with 1/Σ, e−1 and e−3.

Answer to the prediction: 63 % of flights are shorter than one mean free path (1 − e−1), and only 5 % are longer than three. Many short flights and a few long ones is what the histogram shows, whatever Σt is.

A real number: Godiva

For Godiva's uranium (HEU-MET-FAST-001, U-234, U-235 and U-238 at the atom densities of the five-route study, 293.6 K) our port's data path gives, at 1 MeV, a macroscopic total cross section whose reciprocal, the mean free path, is 3.015 cm. The sphere's diameter, 17.48 cm, is almost six mean free paths. At 0.1 MeV it is 1.719 cm. (Infinitely dilute values from Nuclide::xs_at_energy, written by examples/lesson_collision.rs, 2026-10-18; the sphere's radius and densities are vv::godiva.)

Why this is allowed to be so simple

The exponential law is memoryless: a neutron that has flown 2 cm without colliding is, as far as its next collision goes, a brand-new neutron. That is why the code can throw a flight away and draw it again from anywhere (at a boundary, in the next pages; at a virtual collision, in delta tracking) without changing the answer.
